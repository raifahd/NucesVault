Technical & Business Writing (TBW) — Week 1

Technical Communication in the Workplace

Core Texts: Pfeiffer (8th Ed.) • Lannon & Gurak (14th Ed.)

1Workplace Communication & The Rhetorical Situation

Definitions, career stakes, academic vs. workplace writing, document taxonomy

Definition
Workplace Communication: All written and oral communications done on the job (also known as professional, business, or occupational writing).

Career Impact

  • Performance Evaluations: Directly determines promotion and salary reviews.
  • Professional Reputation: Builds credibility among managers, clients, and colleagues.
  • Organizational Productivity: Prevents costly errors and drives marketplace success.
  • Dual Role: In the workplace, you are both a producer and a consumer of technical information.

The Rhetorical Situation (Lloyd Bitzer)

Concept
Rhetorical Situation: The context (audience, problem, setting) that dictates the appropriate content, form, and tone of a message.

Academic Writing vs. Workplace Writing

Feature Academic Writing Workplace Writing
Purpose Display student knowledge to earn a grade. Get work done and enable decisions.
Writer's Knowledge Less than the teacher grading it. More specialized than the reader.
Audience Single teacher (captive evaluator). Multiple busy stakeholders with diverse backgrounds.
Evaluation Criteria Depth, theory, grammar, length. Clarity, conciseness, visual organization.
Visual Elements Used occasionally as secondary aids. Essential for scanning (tables, bullets, bolding).

Document Types & Core Characteristics

  • Correspondence: Memos, routine letters, sales letters, emails.
  • Short Reports: Problem analyses, recommendations, equipment evaluations, progress reports.
  • Long Reports: Project proposals, feasibility studies, formal reports.
  • Other: Manuals, technical presentations, website documentation.
6 Characteristics of Technical Writing
1. Purposeful (goal-driven) • 2. Contextualized (tailored to situation) • 3. Realistic (factual) • 4. Directional (targeted audience) • 5. Outcome-based (drives action) • 6. Interdisciplinary (cross-domain).

Key Takeaways

  • Workplace communication aims to get things done, not just display knowledge.
  • Lloyd Bitzer's Rhetorical Situation requires matching content, form, and tone to the setting.
  • Writers in industry usually have more specialized knowledge than their busy readers.

2The 10 Key Factors, Purpose & Audience

Checklist factors, communication goals, and stakeholder mapping

The 10 Things to Consider

  • 1. Audience: Technical background, role, and operational needs.
  • 2. Purpose: Inform, instruct, troubleshoot, request, or persuade.
  • 3. Complexity: Break dense information into simple, modular ideas.
  • 4. Format: Choose the right medium (email, report, memo, deck).
  • 5. Clarity: Concise, error-free, and unambiguous messaging.
  • 6. Feedback: Enable clear Q&A and confirmation loops.
  • 7. Timeliness: Deliver promptly to avoid project bottlenecks.
  • 8. Visuals: Diagrams, tables, screenshots for fast comprehension.
  • 9. Accessibility: Easy to access and read across all formats.
  • 10. Consistency: Uniform terminology and formatting throughout.

Purpose: Two Main Categories

Technical writing is done by an informed writer conveying needed information to an uninformed reader.

Core Purposes
  • Inform / Instruct: Help others learn, carry out a task, or make a decision.
  • Persuade / Motivate: Reinforce/change attitudes and motivate readers to take action.
Core Guiding Questions
Q1 (Purpose): Am I writing primarily to create a record, provide information, make a request, or persuade?
Q2 (Audience): Who will read what I have written, and what do they already know?

Audience Taxonomy

  • Supervisors / Upper Management: Focus on high-level results, costs, and decision points.
  • Colleagues & Peers: Need technical details, data, and methods.
  • Subordinates: Need clear step-by-step instructions and safety procedures.
  • Clients / Vendors: Require friendly, jargon-free explanations, timelines, and clear deliverables.
  • HR & Committees: Focus on policy, compliance, and formal standards.

Key Takeaways

  • Always identify audience knowledge levels before writing.
  • Writing falls into two main purposes: informing (tasks/decisions) and persuading (action).
  • Check against the 10 factors to ensure complete and effective delivery.

3Tone, The "You" Attitude & Positive Language

Writer's emotional attitude, reader-centered phrasing, and constructive framing

Definition
Tone: The writer's attitude and emotional character toward the reader and the subject matter.

Characteristics of Effective Workplace Tone

Tone should be factual, neutral, sincere, non-discriminative, even/flat, and polite (especially when writing upward or discussing sensitive issues).

The Reader-Centered "You" Attitude

Shift focus from what you/we did to what the reader receives or needs.

Writer-Centered ("I / We") Reader-Centered ("You" Attitude)
"If I can answer any questions, I'll be happy to do so." "If you have any questions, please ask."
"We shipped the order this morning." "Your order was shipped this morning."
"I'm happy to report that we finished the audit." "You will be glad to know the audit is complete."

Positive Language & Constructive Framing

Negative Framing (Blunt / Accusatory) Positive Framing (Solution-Oriented)
"We cannot process your claim because forms are missing." "Your claim can be processed as soon as you complete the forms."
"We do not take phone calls after 3:00 PM on Fridays." "You may reach us by phone on Fridays until 3:00 PM."
"We closed your case because you never sent the info." "Your case will be reactivated as soon as you provide the requested info."
Positive Language: Replacing "No Problem"
Avoid casual phrases like "No problem" (which subtly implies a problem existed). Instead use: "You are welcome," "Happy to help," "Certainly," or "My pleasure."

Key Takeaways

  • Adopt the "You" attitude to emphasize the reader's perspective and benefits.
  • Phrase sentences positively: state what can be done instead of dwelling on negatives.
  • Keep tone polite, objective, and non-threatening.

4ABC Format & Professional Email Guidelines

The Diamond structure and workplace email hygiene

The ABC Diamond Structure

Framework
  • A — Abstract (Top): Brief hook stating purpose, main context, and bottom line.
  • B — Body (Middle): Expansive technical details, data, methodology, and explanations.
  • C — Conclusion (Bottom): Brief summary, next steps, action items, and contact info.

Essential Email Guidelines

  • Subject Line: Must clearly describe the specific topic and purpose.
  • No Flames: Never send abusive, sarcastic, or angry messages.
  • Professional Usernames: Use official names (jsmith@domain.com), not hobby handles.
  • No Emoticons / Text Acronyms: Eliminate smileys and abbreviations (BTW, ASAP, LOL).
  • Cover Note for Attachments: Always describe attached files in the email body.
  • Thoughtful CC: Copy only relevant stakeholders; never misuse "Reply All".
  • Case Sensitivity: Never write in ALL CAPS (shouting) or all lowercase.
Sample

Instructor Email Rubric Checklist

  • Clear subject line (Course + Topic + Student Name + ID)
  • Formal salutation (Dear Professor Ahmed,)
  • Concise body (Goals, background, communication self-assessment)
  • Formal closing and complete contact signature

Key Takeaways

  • The ABC Diamond model keeps documents logically organized from hook to details to conclusion.
  • Workplace emails demand clear subject lines, professional decorum, and attachment context.

5Intercultural Communication & Workplace Ethics

High vs. Low context cultures, Simplified English, and ethical communication

High-Context vs. Low-Context Cultures (Edward T. Hall)

  • High-Context (e.g., Pakistan, Japan): Homogeneous cultures; communication is implicit, subtle, and prioritizes relationships and saving face.
  • Low-Context (e.g., USA, Germany): Diverse cultures; communication is explicit, direct, rule-governed, and open to debate.

Simplified English (Controlled English)

Standardized English (ASD-STE100 / Intecom) to reduce translation and reading errors:

  • Short sentences (<20 words) with simple verb tenses.
  • Consistent terminology (no alternating synonyms).
  • No sports/cultural idioms (e.g., replace "touch base" with "meet").
  • Use visual aids to clarify technical concepts.

Workplace Ethics & 8 Unethical Practices

Deceptive Accuracy
Statements can be technically accurate but misleading (e.g., claiming 0% cholesterol on cookies loaded with trans-fats). Communicators must be honest in overall effect.
Unethical Practice Meaning & Example
1. Suppression of Info Burying dangerous test data or inconvenient truths.
2. Falsification Inventing or altering numbers and records.
3. Over/Understatement Exaggerating positives (51% = "massive majority") or minimizing risks.
4. Selective Misquoting Deleting words to invert or distort original meaning.
5. Subjective Wording Using ambiguous terms like "customary charges" to deceive.
6. Conflict of Interest Steering contracts to family/friends without formal disclosure.
7. Withholding Info Refusing to share data with coworkers to undermine them.
8. Plagiarism Taking credit for someone else's work or ideas.

Key Takeaways

  • High-context cultures focus on relationships and harmony; low-context cultures focus on direct, explicit rules.
  • Simplified English eliminates idioms and ambiguity for global teams.
  • Conflicts of interest require mandatory disclosure and recusal.

Interactive Practice Questions & MCQs

Pre-Email Quiz Bank
Q1: Which of the following is Lloyd Bitzer's "Rhetorical Situation"?

Options:
(A) A method for writing emails in diamond format.
(B) The context of audience and setting requiring appropriate content, form, and tone.
(C) A grammatical rule for passive verbs.
(D) A legal document signed by contractors.

Answer: (B)
Explanation: Bitzer defined the rhetorical situation as the context that governs how a speaker/writer must adapt content, form, and tone to audience expectations.

Q2: How does the writer's knowledge compare to the reader's knowledge in workplace writing vs. academic writing?

Options:
(A) In workplace writing, the writer knows more about the specific topic than the reader; in academic writing, the student writer knows less than the teacher.
(B) In both, the reader always knows more.
(C) In academic writing, the student knows more than the professor.
(D) In workplace writing, the writer and reader always have identical knowledge.

Answer: (A)
Explanation: Academic writers write to demonstrate knowledge to a more knowledgeable evaluator (teacher). Workplace writers write to inform or instruct readers who lack that specific technical insight.

Q3: Which sentence best applies the reader-centered "You" approach?

Options:
(A) "We require you to fill this out so we can update our database."
(B) "I am happy to say that I shipped your laptop today."
(C) "Your laptop has been shipped and will arrive by Tuesday."
(D) "We have decided to change our store policy."

Answer: (C)
Explanation: (C) focuses on the customer's perspective and benefit ("Your laptop... will arrive by Tuesday") rather than company-centric actions.

Q4: Convert this negative sentence into a positive one: "We cannot process your application because you forgot to sign page 2."

Model Answer:
"Your application can be processed as soon as you sign page 2."
Explanation: Positive phrasing states what can be done to resolve the situation rather than placing blame ("you forgot").

Q5: What are the two primary purposes of technical writing?

Answer:
1. To inform/instruct: Help others learn about a subject, perform a task, or make a decision.
2. To persuade/motivate: Reinforce or change attitudes and motivate readers to take action.

Q6: Which of the following is NOT one of the 10 Things to Consider in technical communication?

Options:
(A) Timeliness
(B) Emotional Flattery
(C) Complexity
(D) Consistency

Answer: (B)
Explanation: The 10 factors are Audience, Purpose, Complexity, Format, Clarity, Feedback, Timeliness, Visuals, Accessibility, and Consistency. Flattery is unprofessional.

Q7: Why should a professional replace casual expressions like "No problem" when responding to clients?

Answer:
"No problem" subtly implies that the task was potentially a burden or inconvenience. Replacing it with "You are welcome," "Happy to help," or "Certainly" maintains professional courtesy and positive reassurance.

Q8: Name the 4 main categories of workplace technical documents.

Answer:
1. Correspondence: Memos, letters, emails.
2. Short Reports: Problem analyses, recommendations, progress reports, lab/trip reports.
3. Long Reports: Formal project reports, proposals, feasibility studies.
4. Other: Manuals, technical presentations, specs, websites.

Quick Quiz Recap

  • Understand the Rhetorical Situation and why context matters.
  • Know the 5 key differences between academic and workplace writing.
  • Practice turning "I/We" statements into "You" attitude sentences.
  • Know the 10 factors and the dual purposes of technical communication.
