1Aesthetics, Scales & Coordinate Systems (Chapters 2 & 3)

Grammar of graphics foundations, scale mappings, aspect ratios, Cleveland's 45° banking, and non-linear coordinates.

At its core, data visualization is the systematic mapping of data values onto visual attributes of graphical objects. This concept forms the foundation of Leland Wilkinson's Grammar of Graphics and Hadley Wickham's ggplot2.

Aesthetics & Scales: The Core Duality
  • Aesthetic: A visual property of a graphical mark (geometry) that can encode information. Key aesthetics include: Position ($x, y$), Shape, Size, Color (Hue), Fill, Line width, and Line type (solid, dashed).
  • Scale: A mathematical function that uniquely maps data values from the original data space into visual aesthetic coordinates (e.g., mapping kilograms to pixel heights, or categories to RGB hex codes). Scales can be Continuous (numerical gradients) or Discrete (finite categories).

Variable Types in Data Space

Data Variable Type Mathematical Property Appropriate Aesthetics Wilke Example
Continuous Quantitative Arbitrary real values within a range; measurable intervals. Position ($x,y$), size, color luminance / saturation. Engine displacement (liters), fuel economy (mpg), salary.
Discrete Quantitative Countable integers; discrete jumps between steps. Position, size, number of marks. Number of car cylinders (4, 6, 8), employee count.
Categorical Nominal (Unordered) Discrete buckets with NO intrinsic order or hierarchy. Hue (qualitative), shape (up to ~5), position. Drivetrain (FWD, RWD, 4WD), US geographic regions.
Categorical Ordinal (Ordered) Discrete buckets WITH a meaningful natural ranking. Position, ordered lightness, size. Education level (High School, BSc, MSc, PhD), survey ratings.

Coordinate Systems & Cleveland's 45° Banking Law

Coordinate systems combine two or more spatial scales to define locations in 2D space. The standard is 2D Cartesian coordinates ($x, y$ orthogonal axes).

Aspect Ratio & Banking to 45 Degrees (Cleveland, 1988)

The aspect ratio is the ratio of figure height to width. Changing the aspect ratio changes the visual slope of lines without changing the underlying mathematical data!

Cleveland's 45° Banking Rule: When visualizing time series or functions, choose an aspect ratio such that the average absolute visual orientation of line segments is banked to approximately 45° ($\pm 45^\circ$).

Psychophysical Rationale: The human visual system is most sensitive to differences in slope and changes in rate of growth when orientations hover near 45°. Flat lines (~0°) or near-vertical lines (~90°) mask subtle velocity changes.

Non-Linear Coordinate Systems

  • Logarithmic Scales: A constant linear distance corresponds to multiplication by a constant factor ($10\times$ on base-10, $2\times$ on base-2). Used when values span multiple orders of magnitude or when visualizing ratios. (Note: Transforming the axis coordinates preserves original data labels; transforming raw data changes tick labels to logarithms).
  • Square Root Scales: Scales $x \to \sqrt{x}$. Compresses large numbers less aggressively than log; ideal for counts generated by Poisson processes or surface areas.
  • Polar Coordinates: Specifies points via angle ($\theta$) and radius ($r$). Used for circular phenomena: pie charts (angle = proportion), wind roses, and seasonal cyclical clocks.
Topic 1 High-Yield Takeaways
  • Continuous data maps best to position or luminance; nominal data maps to hue or shape.
  • Aspect ratio directly governs slope perception; bank slopes to ~45° for maximum sensitivity.
  • Log scales are multiplicative; polar coordinates map data to angles and radii.

2Color Design, Scales & Common Pitfalls (Chapters 4 & 19)

The three functional uses, qualitative/sequential/diverging rules, and the four fatal color traps.

Color must always serve an analytical function. Wilke organizes color into three core scale families and identifies the dangerous cognitive traps that ruin otherwise sound visualizations.

Scale Family Analytical Purpose Design Rules & Constraints Standard Palettes
Qualitative Distinguish unordered nominal categories. Colors must be distinct yet perceptually equivalent (equal visual weight). Must NOT imply order (no lightness gradients!). Okabe-Ito (Wilke default, colorblind safe), ColorBrewer Dark2, ggplot2 hue.
Sequential Represent continuous quantitative magnitude. Must vary perceptually uniformly across lightness. Multi-hue scales must follow natural gradients (dark cool/warm → light yellow). ColorBrewer Blues (monochrome), Viridis, Heat, Magma.
Diverging Represent deviation in two directions from a neutral midpoint. Two sequential scales stitched at a light neutral center. Must be strictly balanced in both directions. Midpoint must have domain meaning. Blue-White-Red, Brown-Teal, Pink-YellowGreen.
Accent Highlight focal elements against a background story. Subdued/drab base colors paired with vivid saturated accents. If base colors distract, remove all color from baseline (pure grayscale). ColorBrewer Accent, Grayscale + Accent.

Chapter 19: The Four Fatal Pitfalls of Color Use

Trap 1: Too Many Colors Cognitive Overload

Attempting to distinguish more than 5 to 8 discrete colors fails human working memory. Readers spend all their time cross-referencing the legend.

Remedy: Direct labeling, or group minor categories into "Other".
Trap 2: Rainbow / Jet Colormap False Visual Edges

The classic Jet colormap has non-monotonic luminance (yellow is artificially bright; cyan has sharp bands). It creates false visual boundaries where none exist in data!

Remedy: Monotonically increasing scales like Viridis.
Trap 3: Unintuitive Color Order Brain Friction

Using unordered hues for ordered data, or using colors that contradict cultural conventions (e.g., using red for cold water and blue for hot boiling steam).

Remedy: Align lightness and established cultural associations.
Trap 4: Ignoring Color Deficiency Inaccessible

~8% of men have red-green color vision deficiency (deuteranopia or protanopia). Pairing standard green against standard red makes data completely identical to them!

Remedy: Use Okabe-Ito or test with CVD simulators.
Topic 2 High-Yield Takeaways
  • Qualitative scales require equal visual weight and no implied order (Okabe-Ito).
  • Multi-hue sequential scales must move dark cool/warm → light yellow. Never reverse it.
  • Diverging scales must be balanced symmetrically around a domain-meaningful midpoint.
  • Never use the Rainbow/Jet colormap; never exceed 5–8 categories without direct labels.

3The Principle of Proportional Ink (Chapter 17)

Bergstrom & West's law, linear baseline violations, log amounts vs ratios, and direct area limitations.

When a visual element has a defined physical extent (height, area, volume), human perception instinctively evaluates that extent as the data quantity.

The Principle of Proportional Ink (Bergstrom & West, 2016)

"When a shaded region is used to represent a numerical value, the area of that shaded region should be directly proportional to the corresponding value."

$$\frac{\text{Visual Ink Area}(A)}{\text{Visual Ink Area}(B)} = \frac{\text{Data Value}(A)}{\text{Data Value}(B)}$$

Any visual violation occurs when this proportionality ratio breaks, deceiving the human visual cortex which instinctively interprets surface area as quantity.

The Linear Axis Zero-Baseline Law

In a bar chart on a linear scale, value is encoded by (1) bar endpoint position and (2) total bar length/area.

  • The Truncation Violation (Figure 17-1): When bars start at an arbitrary non-zero baseline (e.g. Hawaii counties starting at $50,000), the endpoint shows the actual value, but the area represents excess over $50k. Because human eyes perceive area/length, a $1.4\times$ income difference looks like an $8\times$ disparity!
  • The Inviolable Law: Bars on a linear scale MUST ALWAYS start at 0.
  • Shaded Line Charts (Figure 17-3): Shading underneath a time-series line (e.g., Facebook stock price) emphasizes distance to the axis. Truncating the axis at $110 makes a modest dip look like a catastrophic company bankruptcy.

Logarithmic Axes: The Amounts vs. Ratios Law

On a log scale, bar area is proportional to $\log(x)$, not $x$. This leads to a strict mathematical bifurcation:

The Log Scale Area Paradox Mathematical Rule
$$\text{Ink Area} \propto \log(x) \neq x \quad\implies\quad \text{On log axes, bar areas violate proportional ink for raw amounts!}$$

Because $\log(0) = -\infty$, any bar origin picked on a logarithmic axis is 100% arbitrary.

Wilke's Log Scale Decision Matrix
  • Amounts on a Log Scale (GDPs of Oceania): NEVER use bars! Zero lies at $-\infty$. Any baseline chosen (e.g., $0.3\text{B}$ or $10^{-9}\text{B}$) is completely arbitrary. Remedy: Use Cleveland Dot Plots (dots with country names placed directly next to dots).
  • Ratios on a Log Scale (Relative to Reference): Bars ARE valid and superior!
    $$\text{Natural Midpoint} = 1.0 \quad (\log 1 = 0) \quad\implies\quad \begin{cases} \text{Ratio } > 1: & \text{bar extends right} \\ \text{Ratio } < 1: & \text{bar extends left} \end{cases}$$

Exam Golden Rule: Linear bars represent amounts → MUST start at 0. Log bars represent ratios → MUST start at 1.

Direct Area Perception: Pie Charts & Treemaps

In pie charts (Figure 17-11) and treemaps (Figure 17-13), wedge angle/area is directly proportional to value, so they satisfy proportional ink. However, human psychophysics is significantly more accurate at judging 1D linear distances along a common scale than 2D areas or angles. Rhode Island county populations are far easier to evaluate accurately on a simple bar chart!

Topic 3 High-Yield Takeaways
  • Shaded area must be directly proportional to data value (Bergstrom & West).
  • Linear bars must start at 0. Truncating creates internal inconsistency between length and coordinate.
  • Never use bars for amounts on log scale; use Cleveland dot plots with names adjacent to dots.
  • Bars on log scale are valid ONLY for ratios, and must start at 1.
  • Pie charts satisfy proportional ink, but 1D bar length perception is far more accurate.

4Time Series, Trends & Mathematical Smoothing (Chapters 13 & 14)

Line charts, connected scatterplots, LOESS 6-step algorithm, cubic splines, eager vs lazy learning, and data straightening.

Visualizing time series requires understanding continuity, autocorrelation, trend estimation, and mathematical data straightening.

Time Series Principles (Chapter 13)

  • Line Graphs: Time flows on the x-axis from left to right. Lines imply continuous temporal evolution and chronological ordering.
  • The Zero-Baseline Exception for Lines: While bars must start at 0, unshaded line charts do NOT have to start at 0 if the goal is to visualize subtle fluctuations (as long as there is no shading underneath!).
  • Connected Scatterplots: Plotting two continuous variables ($x$ vs $y$) where points are connected chronologically by a line. Uniquely reveals cyclical phases and trajectories (e.g. unemployment vs inflation Phillips curves, or vehicle miles vs gas prices).

Smoothing Mechanics: LOESS vs. Cubic Splines (Chapter 14)

Property LOESS (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing) Smoothing Splines (Cubic Splines)
Learning Paradigm Lazy Learning: No single global formula. Recomputed locally for every single query point. Eager Learning: Fits a single global analytic piecewise cubic polynomial once during training.
Mathematical Formulation Weighted Local Least Squares with Tricube Weight Function:
$$W(u) = \begin{cases} (1 - |u|^3)^3 & \text{for } |u| < 1 \\ 0 & \text{for } |u| \ge 1 \end{cases} \quad \text{where } u = \frac{|x_i - x_0|}{\Delta(x_0)}$$
Smoothly tapers weight from 1 at focal point $x_0$ down to 0 at window boundary $\Delta(x_0)$. 		Penalized Residual Sum of Squares (PRSS):
$$\min_{f} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - f(x_i)\right)^2 + \lambda \int [f''(x)]^2 dx \right\}$$
Balances fit fidelity against curve roughness (integrated squared second derivative).
Tuning Parameter Span / Bandwidth ($\alpha$): Proportion of total points in each local window ($0 < \alpha \le 1$).
Example: $\alpha = 0.5$ means the nearest 50% of all observations contribute. 		Roughness Penalty ($\lambda \ge 0$): Global stiffness controller.
Higher $\lambda$ penalizes high curvature $f''(x)$, forcing a smoother curve.
Extreme Behavior Small $\alpha \to 0$ → overfits local noise (high variance).
Large $\alpha \to 1$ → approaches standard global linear regression (high bias). 		$\lambda \to 0$ → exact interpolating spline (passes through every single point).
$\lambda \to \infty$ → perfectly straight Ordinary Least Squares line ($f''(x) = 0$).
Robustness Iterative reweighting using bisquare residuals eliminates outliers. Sensitive to extreme outliers unless explicitly using robust loss functions.

The OLS Exponential Trap & Data Straightening

When data exhibits exponential growth ($y = a \cdot e^{bx}$), fitting a standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) straight line fails catastrophically: residuals grow exponentially, violating homoscedasticity.

The Three Data Straightening Transformations

Linearize non-linear phenomena to satisfy Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) constant variance assumptions:

1. Log-Linear (Exponential) Growth Rate
$$y = a \cdot e^{bx} \iff \ln(y) = \ln(a) + bx$$

Transform: $y \to \ln(y)$ (leave $x$ untransformed).

  • Slope ($b$): Instantaneous continuous fractional growth rate per unit time.
  • Intercept ($\ln a$): Initial baseline quantity at $x = 0$.
  • Exam Note: Straight line on semi-log axis = true exponential growth.
2. Log-Log (Power Law) Scaling Law
$$y = a \cdot x^k \iff \ln(y) = \ln(a) + k \cdot \ln(x)$$

Transform: Both $x \to \ln(x)$ and $y \to \ln(y)$.

  • Slope ($k$): Power-law elasticity / scaling exponent (1% change in $x \to k\%$ in $y$).
  • Intercept ($\ln a$): Normalization constant.
  • Exam Note: Straight line on log-log axis = true power law (e.g. Kleiber's Law).
3. Linear-Log (Logarithmic) Saturation
$$y = a + b \cdot \ln(x)$$

Transform: $x \to \ln(x)$ (leave $y$ untransformed).

  • Slope ($b$): Absolute change in $y$ when $x$ increases by factor of $e$.
  • Behavior: Rapid initial rise that tapers into diminishing marginal returns.
STL Decomposition (The Keeling Curve)

In Chapter 14, Wilke analyzes the atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ Keeling curve using STL Decomposition (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using LOESS):

$$Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$
$T_t$ (Trend): Long-term anthropogenic upward trajectory (> 50 ppm rise over 60 years).
$S_t$ (Seasonal): Annual biological cycle (~8 ppm oscillation from Northern hemisphere summer photosynthesis).
$R_t$ (Remainder): Irregular residual noise / measurement fluctuations (< 1.6 ppm).
Topic 4 High-Yield Takeaways
  • Connected scatterplots reveal cyclical phase relationships across two continuous time series.
  • LOESS is lazy learning with tricube weights and span $\alpha$; Splines are eager learning with roughness penalty $\lambda$.
  • Data straightening linearizes exponential (log-linear) and power law (log-log) curves before regression.
  • STL decomposition splits time series into Trend, Seasonal, and Remainder components using LOESS.

5Geospatial Data, Projections & Choropleths (Chapter 15)

Oblate spheroid, conformal vs equal-area, Mercator vs Goode homolosine, Alaska scaling, and the Choropleth Density Law.

Geospatial figures link data to physical coordinates on Earth. Projecting curved 3D coordinates onto flat 2D planes requires navigating geometric trade-offs.

Spherical Geometry & The Projection Dilemma

  • Coordinates: Longitude (meridians, equal length half-great circles, $0^\circ$ Greenwich, $\pm 180^\circ$ Date Line), Latitude (parallels, parallel to equator, shrink from Equator to $0$ length at poles). Datum: WGS 84 (EPSG:4326).
  • Gauss's Impossibility: Curved surfaces cannot flatten without distortion. Conformal preserves local angles and shapes (Mercator); Equal-Area preserves relative land surface areas (Albers, Goode homolosine).
  • The Mercator Flaw: Conformal cylinder projection; polar areas expand to infinity. Greenland looks bigger than Africa (Africa is actually 14× larger!).
  • US State Standard: Albers Equal-Area (ESRI:102003). Inset Alaska and Hawaii at 100% true scale. Downscaling Alaska to 35% is a deceptive cartographic fraud.

The Choropleth Density Law & Non-Density Exceptions

The Density Law of Choropleth Mapping

Choropleths work best when the mapped variable is a DENSITY (quantity divided by surface area, e.g., people/km²). Because the human visual system equates polygon surface area with total magnitude, shading by density neutralizes area-size bias. Never map raw counts!

The 2 Strict Exceptions for Non-Density Choropleths (e.g. Median Income):

  1. Regions have approximately equal physical size and shape.
  2. Regions are very small relative to the map canvas AND the variable changes on a geographic scale larger than individual regions (e.g., Texas counties).
The State-Level Failure & Cartogram Alternatives

Mapping median income at the state level (Figure 15-14) fails because massive, sparsely populated Alaska visually dominates the graphic.

  • Cartograms: Distort state boundaries proportional to population (Figure 15-15).
  • Cartogram Heatmaps: Represent each state as an identically sized square tile arranged in approximate geographic topology (Figure 15-16). Gives every state equal visual weight!
Topic 5 High-Yield Takeaways
  • Conformal preserves angles/shapes; Equal-Area preserves surface areas.
  • Mercator distorts poles severely (Africa is 14× larger than Greenland).
  • Choropleths must map densities, not raw counts, to counteract polygon area bias.
  • Bin choropleth color scales into 4–6 discrete buckets for readability.
  • Cartogram heatmaps use equal-sized square tiles to eliminate geographic area bias.

6Design Polish, Overplotting & Visualization Pitfalls (Chapters 18–25)

Handling overplotting, redundant coding, data-ink balance, large axis labels, and avoiding line drawings.

The final section of the book covers practical design craftsmanship: resolving visual clutter, balancing context, and avoiding graphic antiquities.

Chapter 18: Handling Overlapping Points (Overplotting)

Overplotting occurs when large datasets or rounded numbers cause many points to plot directly on top of each other, obscuring sample size and distributions.

1. Partial Transparency (Alpha) Solution 1

Make points partially transparent. Overlapping points build up darker ink saturation, revealing density clusters.

2. Jittering Solution 2

Add small random noise to coordinates. Best for discrete, rounded data (e.g. 2.0L engine fuel economy).

3. 2D Histograms (Hexbins) Solution 3

Bin points into 2D bins. Hexagonal bins (hexbin) are superior to rectangular bins because all 6 neighbors are equidistant.

4. 2D Density Contours Solution 4

Estimate 2D kernel density (kde2d) and plot elevation contour lines, analogous to a topographic elevation map.

Chapter 20: Redundant Coding & Direct Labeling

Redundant coding means encoding the exact same categorical variable using two different aesthetics simultaneously (e.g., mapping drivetrain to both color AND shape).

  • Why do it? It ensures the graphic is readable by colorblind viewers, survives black-and-white photocopying, and speeds up cognitive identification.
  • Direct Labeling: Placing category names directly next to data curves/lines instead of in a separate legend box. Eliminates the cognitive penalty of visual back-and-forth scanning!

Chapters 23, 24 & 25: Context, Sizing & Fills

  • Chapter 23 (Balance Data and Context): The data must be the visual hero. Grid lines and backgrounds must be subtle (thin, light gray). Heavy black grids distract and compete with data marks.
  • Chapter 24 (Use Larger Axis Labels): Design figures at the physical scale they will be consumed. Axis titles and tick numbers must remain instantly legible when embedded in slides or papers. If category names are long, rotate the chart into a horizontal bar plot.
  • Chapter 25 (Avoid Line Drawings): Empty wireframe outlines look hollow and outdated. Solid fills define boundaries and surface areas far more clearly. Avoid cross-hatched patterns (they cause optical moiré vibrations). Use violin plots or sina plots instead of raw empty boxplots.
Topic 6 High-Yield Takeaways
  • Combat overplotting with alpha transparency, jittering, hexbin histograms, or 2D contours.
  • Hexagonal bins avoid the directional orientation bias of rectangular bins.
  • Redundant coding (color + shape) protects accessibility and black-and-white printing.
  • Direct labeling eliminates separate legend lookup friction.
  • Keep grid lines light and unobtrusive; use large readable axis labels and solid fills.

Visual extent must scale 1:1 with data value. Mandatory 0-baseline on linear bars.

Balances fit error against total curvature. $\lambda \to 0$: interpolator; $\lambda \to \infty$: OLS straight line.

Transform $y \to \ln(y)$. Straight line slope $b$ represents constant continuous fractional growth rate.

Transform both $x$ and $y$ to logarithms. Straight line slope $k$ is power-law scaling exponent.
