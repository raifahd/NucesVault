    Exploratory Data Analysis

    1Three Modes of Data Analysis

    Confirmatory, Exploratory, and Explanatory

    Data analysis is not a monolith. The approach and visualizations you use depend entirely on what you are trying to achieve. There are three primary modes of data analysis:

    ModePrimary GoalCharacteristicsExample
    Confirmatory (CDA) Statistical inference and hypothesis testing. Relies on probability models (t-dist, normal). Assumes data is a random sample from a hypothetical population. Used to estimate parameters and calculate confidence intervals. Testing if a new drug significantly lowers blood pressure compared to a placebo.
    Exploratory (EDA) Find insights and understand the data context. Creating preliminary visualizations to uncover patterns/trends. Often quick, rough, and used internally by the data scientist. Plotting a quick scatterplot of age vs income to see if any visible correlation exists.
    Explanatory Tell a story to an external audience. Producing polished, presentation-quality figures to communicate specific, interesting findings. Creating an annotated, branded bar chart for an executive board meeting.
    EDA vs Explanatory Visualization
    During EDA, you might generate 100 messy, unpolished plots just to understand what's in the data. Only 1 or 2 of those might reveal something important enough to be cleaned up, annotated, and turned into an Explanatory visualization for stakeholders.

    2The Question-and-Answer Workflow

    A structured approach to Exploratory Data Analysis

    EDA is not just mindlessly plotting variables against each other. It should be an iterative, question-driven process.

    1

    Ask a Domain Question

    Start with specific questions related to the project goal. Don't start with "let's plot X". Start with: "Are global organ donations increasing over time?"

    2

    Choose a Technique

    Select a visualization based on the variables involved in your question. (e.g., For a question about "change over time," select a line plot).

    3

    Iterate and Refine

    Let the initial answers prompt new questions. If the line plot shows a sudden dip in 2020, ask a follow-up question: "Was that dip isolated to one specific country?" Always keep the overall project goal in focus to avoid getting lost in the weeds.

    Common Mistake
    Getting lost during EDA by falling down a rabbit hole of interesting but irrelevant patterns. Always tie your follow-up questions back to the core project goal.

    3Choosing a Visualization Technique

    Matching the plot to the variable types (Step 2 of the workflow)

    The type of visualization you choose depends strictly on the types of variables you are trying to compare.

    1 Numeric

    Histogram / Density

    Shows the distribution of a single continuous variable. Good for spotting skewness or outliers.

    1 Categorical

    Bar Chart

    Shows the frequency or count of categories. (Avoid pie charts unless comparing to a clear whole).

    2 Numeric

    Scatterplot

    Shows the relationship or correlation between two continuous variables.

    1 Numeric + 1 Cat

    Boxplot / Violin Plot

    Compares the distribution of a numeric variable across different categories.

    Time Series

    Line Plot

    Connects numeric data points over sequential time to show trends.

    🎯 Practice Questions

    Q: What is the primary difference between Exploratory and Explanatory Data Analysis?

    A: Audience and Polish. Exploratory analysis is used internally by the data scientist; the visualizations are often rough, numerous, and meant purely to understand the data. Explanatory analysis is for an external audience; the visualizations are heavily polished, annotated, curated, and meant to communicate a specific finding or tell a story.

    Q: Describe the 3 steps of the Q&A workflow for EDA.

    A: 1) Ask a Domain Question (e.g., "Do older houses sell for less?"). 2) Choose a Technique based on variables (e.g., House Age is numeric, Sale Price is numeric → use a scatterplot). 3) Iterate and Refine based on the answer (e.g., if the scatterplot shows a dip but then a rise for very old houses, ask "Are houses older than 100 years considered historical/premium?").

    Q: What is Confirmatory Data Analysis (CDA)?

    A: CDA is the traditional statistical approach focused on hypothesis testing and inference. It relies on probability models (like the normal distribution) and assumes the data is a random sample from a hypothetical population. Its goal is to confirm or reject hypotheses (e.g., calculating p-values or confidence intervals).
