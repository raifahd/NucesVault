Visualizing Data: Mapping Aesthetics & Coordinate Systems

📖
Core Textbook Reference: Claus O. Wilke, Fundamentals of Data Visualization (O'Reilly Media, 2019), Chapter 2: "Visualizing Data: Mapping Data onto Aesthetics" & Chapter 3: "Coordinate Systems and Axes".

1Aesthetics and Types of Data

The fundamental grammar of graphics: converting raw data values into quantifiable visual channels.

Whenever you look at a data graphic—whether it is a sophisticated genomic scatterplot, a financial candlestick chart, or a simple pie chart—the computer has executed a systematic transformation. It took abstract numbers or words stored in memory tables and converted them into physical ink marks on a page or colored photons emitting from pixel clusters on your screen.

Despite their visual diversity, all data visualizations share a unified fundamental grammar: they map data values systematically onto quantifiable, visible features of graphical elements. In data visualization theory, these visual attributes are known as aesthetics.

Definition
Aesthetic: A quantifiable visual property or feature of a graphical element (such as position, shape, size, color, line width, or line type) that can be varied systematically to represent data values.
Intuitive translation: The "visual dial" you twist (like making a dot higher, wider, or redder) to reflect a change in your data.

The Core Aesthetic Channels

Every graphical mark in 2D space possesses physical traits that your visual cortex processes pre-attentively (instantaneously without conscious calculation). Wilke identifies the core aesthetic channels shown in the visual card below:

🎨 Aesthetic Channels & Their Data Capabilities

Aesthetics are categorized by whether they can represent continuous quantitative gradients, or are strictly confined to discrete categorical boundaries.

1. Position (x, y) Continuous & Discrete

Horizontal (x) and vertical (y) location. The single most accurate aesthetic for human spatial perception.

2. Size / Area Continuous & Discrete

Length, radius, or bubble area. Excellent for continuous magnitudes (e.g. population, GDP, weight).

3. Color (Hue & Lum) Continuous & Discrete

Continuous gradients (luminance/saturation) or qualitative palettes (distinct hues for categories).

4. Line Width Continuous & Discrete

Thickness of paths or contours. Can represent network traffic volume, flow rate, or road capacity.

5. Shape Discrete Only

Circles, triangles, squares, crosses. Cannot represent continuous numbers because shapes lack a natural order or continuous intermediate states.

6. Line Type Discrete Only

Solid, dashed, dotted, dash-dot. Strictly categorical; there is no intuitive mathematical half-step between dashed and dotted.

High-Yield Exam Trap: Continuous vs. Discrete Aesthetics
Shape and Line Type can NEVER represent continuous quantitative data!
Why? Because continuous data demands arbitrarily fine intermediate values (e.g. between 4.1 and 4.2). What visual shape sits mathematically halfway between a square and an open triangle? None. If a question asks which aesthetic is unsuitable for a continuous variable like temperature or weight, the answer is Shape or Line Type.

The Spectrum of Data Types

Before you can choose which aesthetic channel to activate, you must precisely classify the variables in your dataset. In data analysis and statistics, variables are not just "numbers versus text." Wilke establishes a precise taxonomy:

Quantitative (Numerical) Numbers

  • Continuous: Real numbers where arbitrarily fine intermediates exist.
    Examples: Height (1.75 m), Temperature (72.4°F), Time duration (50.512s), Speed (88.4 mph).
  • Discrete: Countable units where fractional intermediates do not exist in the dataset.
    Examples: Number of passengers in a car (1, 2, 3, 4), Hospital beds occupied, Exam score integers.

Qualitative (Categorical) Factors

  • Unordered Factor (Nominal): Discrete categories without any natural mathematical or hierarchical sequence.
    Examples: Pet type (dog, cat, fish), Location (Chicago, San Diego, Houston).
  • Ordered Factor (Ordinal): Discrete categories possessing an inherent ranking or sequential order.
    Examples: Customer rating (poor < fair < good < excellent), Months of year (Jan, Feb, Mar).

Temporal (Date / Time) Hybrid

  • Calendar Dates & Timestamps: Specific points in time (Jan 5, 2024, 08:30:15 AM). Can be continuous (seconds elapsed) or discrete (calendar day, quarter).
  • Cyclical / Generic Dates: Repeating periods without a year (e.g. July 4th, Spring, Hour 14:00).

Free-Form Text Qualitative

  • Unstructured Text: Open-ended comments, prose (e.g. "The quick brown fox jumps..."). Typically treated as qualitative labels or processed into discrete word frequencies.
Definition
Factor and Levels: In data science (originating in R and statistics), a variable holding qualitative categorical data is called a factor, and each individual unique category within that factor is called a level.
Example: Variable `Weather_Condition` is a factor; its levels are `['Sunny', 'Rainy', 'Cloudy', 'Snowy']`.

Whenever you see an ID code containing numbers (e.g. Student ID 210984, Station ID USW00014819, Zip Code 90210), never classify it as quantitative numerical! IDs are categorical factors (qualitative nominal) because calculating the mean or standard deviation of student IDs is mathematically nonsensical.
Practice Questions for Topic 1
Easy: Can you map an employee's salary ($45,250.75) to the "Shape" aesthetic in a scatterplot? Why or why not?

Answer: No. Salary is a quantitative continuous variable requiring infinitely fine intermediate values. "Shape" is a strictly discrete aesthetic (e.g. circle, square, triangle) with no mathematical ordering or intermediate gradations. Mapping a continuous variable to shape is invalid and will cause an error or unintelligible visualization.

Medium: What is the technical difference between a Factor and an Ordered Factor in data visualization?

Answer: Both represent qualitative categorical data. In a standard (unordered) factor, the levels have no intrinsic ranking (e.g., [cat, dog, bird] or [Chicago, Houston]), meaning they can be sorted in any arbitrary order on an axis. In an ordered factor, the levels possess an inherent, fixed hierarchy (e.g., [Freshman, Sophomore, Junior, Senior] or [Jan, Feb, Mar]) that must be preserved on the visual axis to avoid misleading the reader.

Hard: A dataset contains the values: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0. Can this variable be legitimately classified as Quantitative Discrete? Explain Wilke's subtle distinction.

Answer: Yes! As Wilke explicitly notes in Table 2-1, discrete numbers are most commonly integers, but not necessarily. If in the given operational context intermediate values (such as 0.75 or 1.22) cannot physically or logically exist (e.g., shoe sizes that only come in half-size increments, or camera lens f-stop intervals), the numbers represent discrete units and must be treated as discrete data.

Key Takeaways: Aesthetics & Data Types

  • Aesthetics: Every graphical mark is defined by aesthetic channels: position (x, y), shape, size, color, line width, and line type.
  • Continuous vs. Discrete Aesthetics: Position, size, color, and line width can encode continuous gradients. Shape and line type can only encode discrete categories.
  • Data Types Spectrum: Data is divided into Quantitative (Continuous vs Discrete), Qualitative (Unordered vs Ordered Factors), Date/Time, and Free-Form Text.
  • Factor & Levels: Categorical variables are factors; their unique values are levels. Ordered factors demand strict visual sequencing.

2Scales and Multi-Aesthetic Data Mapping

How scales create unambiguous 1-to-1 links between data values and visual space, and how to encode high-dimensional data.

Knowing what aesthetic channels exist is only half the battle. To actually draw a graphic, the software must know which specific data value maps to which specific visual coordinate or color shade. That translation bridge is called a scale.

Definition
Scale: A formal mathematical mapping function that connects data values to aesthetic values.
The Golden Invertibility Rule: A scale must be strictly one-to-one (bijective): for every unique data value, there is exactly one visual aesthetic value, and for every aesthetic value, there is exactly one data value.
Intuitive translation: If two different temperatures mapped to the identical color red, or if 50°F mapped to two different heights on the y-axis, the chart would be ambiguous and scientifically useless.

🔄 The One-to-One Scale Mapping Architecture

A scale takes input from the Data Domain and projects it onto the Visual Aesthetic Range. The mapping must be invertible in both directions.

Scientific Rule
Stone-Albers Szafir-Setlur Area Saliency Rule (2014): When continuous quantitative data is mapped to color, the human visual system requires sufficiently large colored areas (like thick blocks or tiles) to accurately perceive and compare subtle differences in luminance and hue. Practice Questions for Topic 2
Easy: Why must every visual scale be strictly one-to-one (invertible)?

Answer: If a scale is not one-to-one, multiple distinct data values would produce the exact same visual mark, or a single data value would produce ambiguous marks. The reader would be unable to decode the graphic back into true data values.

Medium: Why did Wilke switch from thin lines to colored squares (tiles) when moving temperature from the y-axis to the color aesthetic?

Answer: Based on the perceptual research of Stone, Albers Szafir, and Setlur (2014), the human eye is poor at discerning fine color/luminance gradations across tiny, thin lines. To convey quantitative differences effectively through color, the graphical elements must occupy a sufficiently large spatial area (like tiles or heatmap squares).

Hard: In the 5-variable car scatterplot, could we swap "Weight" to the Shape channel and "Number of Cylinders" to the Size channel? Explain.

Answer: Weight is a continuous quantitative variable. As established in Chapter 2, Shape cannot represent continuous data because there are no natural continuous intermediate shapes. Number of cylinders, however, has discrete values (4, 6, 8) and can easily be represented by size or shape. Therefore, swapping weight to shape would destroy the visualization.

Key Takeaways: Scales & Multi-Aesthetic Mapping

  • Scales as Bijective Bridges: Scales create an unambiguous, one-to-one link between data space and visual aesthetic space.
  • Continuous vs. Discrete Position Scales: Discrete position scales place levels at equal spacing; ordered levels must follow their hierarchy, while unordered levels should be sorted by a meaningful metric for aesthetic clarity.
  • Area Saliency for Color: Quantitative color scales require large visual areas (like heatmap squares) rather than hair-thin lines.
  • Multi-Dimensional Encoding: Up to 5+ variables can be combined (x, y, color, size, shape), but shape must remain strictly discrete.

3Cartesian Coordinates, Units & Aspect Ratios

Geometric arrangements of position scales, aspect ratio psychology, and invariance under linear transformations.

To position marks on a canvas, you must define position scales. But position scales do not float in a vacuum; they exist within a defined geometric layout. The combination of position scales and their relative spatial orientation is known as a coordinate system.

Definition
Coordinate System: The combination of a set of position scales and their relative geometric arrangement in visual space.
Cartesian Coordinate System: The standard 2D coordinate system consisting of two straight axes (conventionally $x$ horizontal and $y$ vertical) intersecting orthogonally at a 90° right angle, forming an equidistant grid covering a specified numerical range.

Data Units and the Role of the Grid

In pure mathematics, coordinates are abstract real numbers $(x, y)$. In real-world data science, coordinates come with physical units: degrees Fahrenheit, kilograms, miles per hour, dollars, or days.

In a Cartesian coordinate system, the grid lines along an axis correspond to discrete steps in those data units (for example, a grid line every 10°F, or every 5 kilometers). Any data points falling outside the minimum and maximum axis limits are clipped and discarded.

Aspect Ratio Dynamics: The Story Told by Slopes

What happens when the two axes represent two completely different units (e.g. Temperature in °F on the vertical axis and Time in Months on the horizontal axis)?

Because degrees and months have no shared physical conversion, there is no mathematically mandated aspect ratio. You are free to stretch or compress the horizontal axis relative to the vertical axis. However, how you stretch the plot dramatically alters how human beings perceive the data:

📐 Aspect Ratio Perception Demo (Wilke Figure 3-2)

The exact same annual temperature curve for Houston, TX, displayed across three different aspect ratios.

(a) Tall & Narrow

Emphasizes extreme vertical swings. Slopes appear steep and volatile.

(b) Balanced (Banked to ~45°)

(c) Short & Wide

De-emphasizes vertical changes. The curve appears sluggish and flat.

The Golden Exception: Same Units Require 1:1 Aspect Ratio!
If both the x and y axes measure the same physical quantity in the same units (for example, plotting Houston Temperature in °F against San Diego Temperature in °F, or Height in cm vs Arm Span in cm), you MUST enforce a 1:1 aspect ratio where grid lines form perfect squares!
Why? Because if 10 degrees along the x-axis measures 2 cm on paper, but 10 degrees along the y-axis measures 4 cm, the 45-degree diagonal line of equality ($y = x$) will be artificially rotated and distorted, deceiving the viewer about which city is actually hotter!

Invariance Under Linear Transformations

What happens if you convert your temperature data from Fahrenheit to Celsius? Or convert distances from miles to kilometers?

A unit change is a linear transformation of the form:

y' = a · y + b

For Celsius conversion: $C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32) = \frac{5}{9}F - \frac{160}{9}$. Here $a = 5/9$ and $b = -17.78$.

Core Principle
Invariance Under Linear Transformations: Cartesian coordinate systems are fundamentally invariant under linear transformations. If you scale or shift the numbers linearly and adjust the axis tick marks accordingly, the relative visual geometry, the slopes, and the scatter of points remain 100% identical.

In data visualization research (William S. Cleveland), when plotting a single time-series or trend line where you have freedom of aspect ratio, the recommended best practice is banking to 45°: choose an aspect ratio such that the average orientation of line segments centers around 45 degrees. Human perception of rate differences is maximized when slopes are near 45°.
Practice Questions for Topic 3
Easy: Why is a Cartesian coordinate system described as "orthogonal"?

Answer: Because the x and y axes meet at right angles (perpendicular, exactly 90 degrees) to each other, allowing each location to be uniquely specified by independent horizontal and vertical coordinates.

Medium: You are plotting Car Weight (lbs) on the x-axis and Fuel Efficiency (mpg) on the y-axis. Must the aspect ratio be 1:1? Why or why not?

Answer: No, it should not be constrained to 1:1. Pounds and miles-per-gallon represent two completely different physical dimensions with no direct unit conversion. You are free to pick an aspect ratio that makes the relationship clear and readable (ideally banking slopes to ~45°). A 1:1 ratio is only required when both axes measure the same unit.

Hard: Prove conceptually why converting a dataset from miles to kilometers does not alter the appearance of a Cartesian scatter plot.

Answer: Converting miles to kilometers is a linear transformation: $\text{km} = 1.60934 \times \text{miles}$. In a Cartesian coordinate system, multiplying all data points by a constant simply scales the position values uniformly. When the axis ticks are relabeled to reflect kilometers, the relative spacing between every pair of points and the visual aspect ratio remain mathematically invariant.

Key Takeaways: Cartesian Coordinates & Aspect Ratios

  • Cartesian System: Orthogonal axes meeting at 90° with equidistant grid units covering defined numerical limits.
  • Aspect Ratio Flexibility: When axes measure different physical units, stretching or compressing the aspect ratio changes perceived slope/volatility.
  • Same Units Rule: When both axes measure the same quantity, the aspect ratio must be 1:1 (square grid) to prevent visual distortion.
  • Linear Invariance: Cartesian coordinates are invariant under linear transformations ($y' = ay + b$); changing units (°F to °C) preserves exact data geometry.

4Nonlinear Axes: Logarithmic and Square-Root Scales

When linear spacing fails: mastering multiplication geometry, the Texas county ratio case study, and zero boundaries.

In a standard linear Cartesian coordinate system, an even spacing in data units corresponds to an even spacing in visual distance. A step from 10 to 20 takes up the exact same physical space on screen as a step from 90 to 100.

While linear scales provide an accurate baseline, linear scales catastrophically fail in two widespread scenarios:

  1. When data spans multiple orders of magnitude (e.g. comparing populations of 80 people vs. 4,000,000 people).
  2. When data has been obtained via multiplication or division (such as growth rates, financial returns, or population ratios).

The Logarithmic Scale: Linear in Multiplication

Definition
Logarithmic Scale (Log Scale): A nonlinear position scale where equal visual step sizes correspond to multiplication by a fixed constant factor (e.g. $\times 10$ or $\times 2$), rather than addition of a constant amount.
Mathematical Foundation: $\log_{10}(1) = 0$, $\log_{10}(10) = 1$, $\log_{10}(100) = 2$, $\log_{10}(1000) = 3$. Each unit distance on the axis is a tenfold increase ($10^x$).

📏 Linear vs. Logarithmic Spacing (Wilke Figure 3-4)

Notice where the values 1, 3.16, 10, 31.6, and 100 fall on both scales. The numbers 3.16 and 31.6 are exactly halfway because $10^{0.5} \approx 3.16$.

(a) Linear Scale (Additive: +25 per grid step) Heavy clustering near 0!
(b) Logarithmic Scale (Multiplicative: ×10 per major interval) Evenly spaced geometric steps!

The 3.16 Mystery Explained: In decimal log scales, the midpoint between 1 ($10^0$) and 10 ($10^1$) on a log axis is $10^{0.5} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$. Similarly, between 10 ($10^1$) and 100 ($10^2$), the halfway point is $10^{1.5} = 10 \times \sqrt{10} \approx 31.62$.

Exam Trap: Axis Title on Log Scales
Never title your axis "$\log(\text{Variable})$" when plotting on a logarithmic scale!
Mathematically, plotting log-transformed data on a linear axis is identical to plotting raw data on a log axis. However, the correct axis label for a logarithmic scale is the name of the variable itself (e.g. "Population" or "Population (log scale)"), and the tick marks should display the actual numbers (e.g., 1, 10, 100 or 10⁰, 10¹, 10²), NOT 0, 1, 2. This relieves the reader's cognitive burden.

Why Ratios MUST Be Displayed on a Log Scale

When data is obtained by division (e.g. County Population divided by Median Population), the natural midpoint is 1.0 (representing an exact match with the median).

  • A value of 10 represents 10 times larger than median ($1 \times 10$).
  • A value of 0.1 represents 10 times smaller than median ($1 / 10$).

On a linear scale, the distance from 1 to 10 is 9 units, while the distance from 1 to 0.1 is only 0.9 units! Thus, a linear scale gives a 10-fold increase ten times more visual prominence than a 10-fold decrease!

On a log scale, multiplication and division are symmetric: $\log_{10}(10) - \log_{10}(1) = 1$, and $\log_{10}(1) - \log_{10}(0.1) = 1$. The distances are identical!

The Zero Dilemma: Why 0 Cannot Exist on a Log Scale

What happens if a county has a population of 0?

Crucial Mathematical Fact
The value 0 can NEVER appear on a logarithmic scale!
$$\log(0) = -\infty$$ To reach zero starting from 1, you would have to divide by 10 infinitely many times ($1 \div 10 \div 10 \div 10 \cdots$). Zero lies infinitely far away in negative infinity. Any data points with value 0 or negative values will either crash your plotting software or be dropped silently!

The Square-Root Scale: Properties & Two Critical Flaws

When data contains zeros or when values represent physical 2D areas, some analysts turn to a square-root scale ($y' = \sqrt{y}$).

Just like a log scale, a square-root scale compresses large numbers into a smaller visual space. Unlike a log scale, it natively accommodates the number 0 ($\sqrt{0} = 0$).

Definition
Square-Root Scale: A nonlinear scale where position is proportional to $\sqrt{x}$. Even spacing on the scale corresponds to the squares of integers: $0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49$.
Natural Domain: Geographic land area! Because $\text{Area} \propto \text{Length}^2$, plotting state areas on a square-root scale directly reflects linear extent (how many hours it takes to drive across the state from east to west).
⚠️ Wilke's Two Major Critiques of Square-Root Scales (High Exam Probability)
  1. No Constant Unit Step Meaning: On a linear scale, a unit step is always $+C$. On a log scale, a unit step is always $\times C$. On a square-root scale, the meaning of a unit step depends on where you start! Going from 0 to 1 adds 1 unit, but going from 9 to 16 adds 7 units for the exact same physical visual jump!
  2. Unintuitive Tick Mark Dilemma: If you place ticks at equal visual intervals, they appear at weird square numbers ($0, 4, 25, 49, 81$), which confuses readers. If you place them at nice round numbers ($10, 20, 30$), the ticks are crowded densely at the bottom and spread far apart at the top.
Practice Questions for Topic 4
Easy: Why can the number zero never be plotted on a logarithmic scale?

Answer: Because the logarithm of zero is mathematically undefined ($\lim_{x \to 0^+} \log(x) = -\infty$). On a log scale, going down requires successive divisions; you can never reach zero through finite divisions.

Medium: In Wilke's linear vs. log scale demonstration, why were 3.16 and 31.6 chosen as intermediate tick marks?

Answer: Because on a base-10 logarithmic scale, the geometric halfway point between 1 ($10^0$) and 10 ($10^1$) is $10^{0.5} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$. Similarly, the halfway point between 10 ($10^1$) and 100 ($10^2$) is $10^{1.5} \approx 31.62$.

Hard: Why is a square-root scale preferred over a linear scale when comparing the land areas of states to estimate driving travel times?

Answer: Land area scales with the square of linear distance ($\text{Area} \propto \text{distance}^2$). Driving across a state depends on linear distance (miles from border to border), not surface square mileage. Taking the square root of the land area ($\sqrt{\text{Area}}$) restores the linear dimension, making visual bar lengths proportional to border-to-border driving time.

Key Takeaways: Nonlinear Axes (Log & Sqrt)

  • Log Scales are Multiplicative: Equal visual spacing represents multiplication by a fixed base ($\times 10$). Ideal for spanning wide orders of magnitude.
  • Mandatory for Ratios: Ratios must always be plotted on a log scale so that deviations above the midpoint (1.0) and below 1.0 are visually symmetric.
  • The Zero Barrier: $\log(0) = -\infty$. Zero and negative values are strictly prohibited on logarithmic scales.
  • Square-Root Scale Trade-Off: Handles zero and naturally models square geographic areas, but suffers from non-uniform step sizes and awkward tick mark placement.

5Curved Coordinates and Map Projections

Polar coordinates for cyclical time phenomena and navigating the distortion trilemma of geospatial cartography.

Every coordinate system examined so far used two straight axes intersecting at a 90° angle. However, not all physical data lives on a flat, infinite Cartesian plane. In many scientific domains, axes must bend and curve.

Polar Coordinate Systems: The Geometry of Cycles

Definition
Polar Coordinate System: A 2D coordinate system where each point's position is determined by an angle ($\theta$) relative to a reference axis and a radial distance ($r$) from the center origin point.
Key Feature: The angle axis is circular and wraps around: $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$) meet at the exact same physical location!

Polar coordinates are the natural mathematical choice for data of a periodic or cyclical nature, where the end of the scale logically connects back to the beginning.

  • Calendar Days in a Year: December 31st is the final day of the year, but it is also exactly one day before January 1st!
  • 24-Hour Clocks: 23:59 wraps directly into 00:00.
  • Compass Headings / Wind Directions: 359° is adjacent to 0° (North).

🔄 Cartesian Split vs. Polar Continuity (Wilke Figures 2-3 & 3-10)

How plotting annual temperature normals in Polar Coordinates solves the "December 31st Discontinuity" present in standard Cartesian plots.

Cartesian Plot: Broken Winter Continuum

In a Cartesian line plot, Jan 1 is at the far left edge and Dec 31 is at the far right edge. Because they are on opposite sides of the paper, the human visual system fails to see that late December and early January are continuous winter weather!

Polar Coordinates: Seamless Seasonal Loop

Days arranged radially (angle $\theta$); Temperature represented by radial distance ($r$) from center.

Wilke's Observation: In the polar visualization, it becomes immediately obvious that Death Valley, Houston, and San Diego share remarkably similar, mild temperatures throughout late fall, winter, and early spring.

Geospatial Data: The Map Projection Dilemma

When visualizing geospatial coordinates, locations on Earth are defined by longitude (east-west) and latitude (north-south).

The Flat Earth Cartography Trap
The Earth is an approximate 3D sphere (oblate spheroid). You cannot flatten a 3D sphere onto a 2D flat sheet of paper or screen without creating mathematical distortions!
Simply treating Longitude as $x$ and Latitude as $y$ in a standard Cartesian grid (the Plate Carrée / Equirectangular projection) severely stretches and blows up landmasses near the North and South Poles. Antarctica and Greenland appear massive, while equatorial continents like Africa are compressed!

The Distortion Trilemma & Projection Comparison

Every map projection represents a compromise among three competing geometric properties:

  • Preserving Area (Equal-Area): Ensuring that equal square inches on the map represent equal square miles on Earth.
  • Preserving Shape / Angles (Conformal): Ensuring that local angles, shapes, and compass headings remain true.
  • Continuity: Keeping continents connected as a continuous unified graphic without tears or splits.
1. Cartesian Lat/Long (Equirectangular)
Type: Cylindrical
Area: Massively Distorted
Angles: Distorted at poles

Treats longitude/latitude as straight grid lines. Severe polar inflation; never recommended for scientific maps.

2. Interrupted Goode Homolosine
Type: Equal-Area (Pseudocylindrical)
Area: 100% Preserved True Area
Angles: Moderate distortion

Slices the oceans into lobes ("orange peel"). Perfectly preserves true relative continental sizes, at the cost of splitting oceans and Antarctica.

3. Robinson Projection
Type: Compromise (Pseudocylindrical)
Area: Balanced / Minor Distortion
Angles: Balanced / Pleasing Curves

Balances angular and area distortions without being strictly equal-area or conformal. Historically adopted by National Geographic.

4. Winkel Tripel Projection
Type: Compromise (Modified Azimuthal)
Area: Minimized Distortion
Angles: Minimized Distortion

Gold standard for world maps. Averages the equirectangular and Aitoff projections to achieve the lowest overall distortion across area, direction, and distance.

Practice Questions for Topic 5
Easy: What are the two coordinates that define a point in a polar coordinate system?

Answer: An angle ($\theta$, measuring angular direction around the circular axis) and a radial distance ($r$, measuring the straight-line distance from the central origin point).

Medium: Why does plotting annual weather normals in a polar coordinate system reveal winter temperature patterns better than a Cartesian plot?

Answer: Weather normals are cyclical; December 31st is immediately adjacent to January 1st. In a Cartesian coordinate system, Dec 31 and Jan 1 are severed and placed at opposite edges of the canvas. In a polar system, the angle wraps continuously in a circle, displaying the late-fall to early-spring weather as an unbroken visual continuum.

Hard: If your objective is to produce a thematic map comparing the proportion of forest cover per country, why is an equal-area projection like Goode Homolosine mandatory over Mercator?

Answer: In thematic and choropleth maps comparing land-based quantities (like forest cover percentage), the visual area occupied by a country directly influences the reader's perception of magnitude. The Mercator projection vastly inflates high-latitude regions (making Greenland appear equal in size to Africa, when Africa is actually 14 times larger). An equal-area projection ensures that visual proportions match physical geographical reality.

Key Takeaways: Curved & Geospatial Coordinates

  • Polar Coordinates ($\theta, r$): Circular angle plus radial radius. Natural choice for periodic, cyclical data where the end loops into the start (calendar days, clock hours, wind directions).
  • The December 31st Discontinuity: Cartesian axes artificially split the winter season; polar coordinates unite Dec 31 and Jan 1.
  • Map Projection Trilemma: Flattening the 3D spherical Earth onto a 2D surface unavoidably distorts area, angles/shapes, or continuity.
  • Projection Champions: Cartesian lat/long distorts polar areas severely; Goode Homolosine preserves true area via lobes; Robinson and Winkel Tripel provide elegant balance.

4. The 10 Deadly Exam Traps

⚡ 10 Subtle Trap Questions Often Found on Data Visualization Quizzes:
  1. Trap 1: "Can shape represent an integer discrete count like number of children?" — Yes! Shape can represent discrete categories or discrete integer groups (e.g. 1, 2, 3+), but CANNOT represent continuous numbers.
  2. Trap 2: "Is an ID code like USW00014819 a numerical variable because it has numbers?" — No! It is an unordered factor (qualitative nominal). Numerical operations on IDs are meaningless.
  3. Trap 3: "What happens if you feed value 0 into a log-transformed axis?" — Mathematical crash ($-\infty$) or point dropped from the plot.
  4. Trap 4: "What is the correct label for a logarithmic axis?" — The name of the variable (e.g. "Revenue ($)"), NOT "log(Revenue)"!
  5. Trap 5: "If a scale is NOT one-to-one, what goes wrong?" — The graphic becomes fundamentally ambiguous (multiple data values yield the same mark, or vice-versa).
  6. Trap 6: "When must a Cartesian aspect ratio strictly be 1:1 (square grid)?" — Only when both axes measure the same unit (e.g. °F vs °F or meters vs meters).
  7. Trap 7: "Does converting from Fahrenheit to Celsius alter the visual slope or shape of a scatterplot?" — No! Cartesian coordinates are strictly invariant under linear transformations.
  8. Trap 8: "Why are ratios displayed on log scales rather than linear scales?" — Because on a log scale, doubling ($\times 2$) and halving ($\div 2$) are visually symmetric around 1.0. Linear scales distort ratios > 1.
  9. Trap 9: "What is the primary flaw of a square-root scale compared to linear or log scales?" — A unit physical step has no constant meaning (its value depends on the starting point).
  10. Trap 10: "Why is the Mercator projection invalid for choropleth thematic maps?" — It severely inflates high-latitude surface areas, misleading viewers about true geographical proportions.
