Color Scales & The Principle of Proportional Ink

📖
Required Textbook Reference: Claus O. Wilke, Fundamentals of Data Visualization, Chapter 4 (Color Scales, pp. 27–35) and Chapter 17 (The Principle of Proportional Ink, pp. 207–217). Bergstrom & West (2016).

1Three Core Uses of Color & Qualitative Color Scales

The foundational taxonomy of color in visualization and the strict conditions for unordered categorical data.

Color is one of the most expressive aesthetics in visual communication, but it is also the easiest to misuse. Claus O. Wilke establishes that before picking any hue, a designer must answer one fundamental question: What specific function is color performing in this graphic? Color is never decorative; it is a mathematical mapping.

The Three Fundamental Use Cases for Color

In data visualization, color serves exactly three distinct analytical functions:

  1. To Distinguish: Differentiate discrete items or categories that have no inherent ranking or numerical order (uses Qualitative scales).
  2. To Represent Data Values: Encode quantitative magnitudes, continuous measurements, or directional deviations (uses Sequential or Diverging scales).
  3. To Highlight: Guide the reader's attention to a selective subset of focal data points or categories against a subdued backdrop (uses Accent scales).

Intuitive Summary: Distinguish separates nominal buckets; Represent plots numbers; Highlight tells a story.

Qualitative Color Scales (Nominal Data)

When data points represent discrete categories without an intrinsic order—such as different countries on a map, manufacturing brands, or US geographic regions—we map them using a qualitative color scale.

Qualitative Color Scale: The Two Inviolable Conditions

A qualitative scale contains a finite set of distinct colors that must satisfy two simultaneous conditions:

  • Condition 1: Visual Distinctness: Colors must be easily distinguishable from one another by normal and color-deficient observers.
  • Condition 2: Perceptual Equivalence: No single color should stand out relative to the others (no color possesses higher visual weight, luminance, or extreme saturation), and the colors must not create the impression of an ordered sequence (such as getting progressively darker or lighter).
The False-Order Trap in Qualitative Scales

If you use a sequence of blues that get successively lighter to represent categorical items (e.g., Apple, Samsung, Google), the viewer's brain automatically perceives a hierarchy or ranking (e.g., Apple is "more" or "better" than Google). Unordered categories demand unordered, equal-luminance hues!

Book Case Study: US Population Growth by Region (2000–2010)

In Figure 4-2, Wilke displays the population growth percentage of all 50 US states, sorted by growth rate, and colors the bars by Geographic Region (West, South, Midwest, Northeast).

  • Why a qualitative scale? The four regions have no inherent mathematical ranking (West is not mathematically "greater" than Northeast).
  • Result: States in the West and South immediately group together at the top of the chart with the highest growth rates (Nevada, Arizona, Utah, Texas), whereas Midwest and Northeast states cluster at the bottom (Michigan actually experienced a decrease).
Exam Pro Tip: Okabe-Ito is the Gold Standard

If an exam asks for the default qualitative color scale used by Claus O. Wilke throughout Fundamentals of Data Visualization, the answer is the Okabe-Ito scale (Okabe & Ito, 2008). It was mathematically designed to be unambiguous under all forms of Color Vision Deficiency (deuteranopia, protanopia, tritanopia).

Practice Questions (Topic 1)

Q1: Why is a sequence of colors transitioning from light blue to dark blue inappropriate for nominal categories?

Answer: It violates Condition 2 of qualitative scales. Succesively darker colors create the perceptual illusion of an inherent rank or quantitative hierarchy among categories that have no actual order.

Q2: What two conditions must all qualitative color scales satisfy according to Chapter 4?

Answer: (1) The colors must be clearly distinct from each other; (2) The colors must be perceptually equivalent, meaning no single color stands out and they do not imply an ordered progression.

Q3: Which qualitative scale is the Wilke textbook default, and why was it chosen?

Answer: The Okabe-Ito scale. It was chosen because its 8 colors are universally distinguishable by individuals with normal vision as well as those with deuteranopia, protanopia, and tritanopia.

2Representing Data Values: Sequential & Diverging Color Scales

Encoding numerical magnitudes, gradients, perceptual uniformity, and the mathematics of neutral midpoints.

When color represents a quantitative numerical variable—such as income, temperature, vehicle speed, or density—it must convey two mathematical properties simultaneously: (1) Order (which value is larger or smaller) and (2) Distance (how far apart two values are).

Sequential Color Scale & Perceptual Uniformity

A sequential color scale maps a continuous numerical range onto a gradient of colors that progress smoothly in visual lightness/luminance.

The Perceptual Uniformity Law: A sequential scale must be perceived to vary uniformly across its entire range. An equal numerical difference ($\Delta x$) anywhere along the scale must produce an equal perceived difference ($\Delta E$) in color.

Single-Hue vs. Multi-Hue Sequential Scales

Sequential scales can be built using a single hue or multiple hues:

  • Single-Hue (Monochromatic): Varies luminance within a single hue (e.g., ColorBrewer Blues: from very light blue to deep navy blue). Highly effective, simple, and colorblind-safe.
  • Multi-Hue: Varies both hue and luminance simultaneously (e.g., Heat: dark red to light yellow; Viridis: dark purple/blue via green to light yellow). Multi-hue scales provide wider dynamic range and sharper contrast for fine gradations.
The Natural Color Gradient Law

Multi-hue sequential scales must follow color gradients found in nature—specifically progressing from dark saturated cool/warm tones (dark red, dark blue, dark purple) to bright, light tones (light yellow, pale green).

Fatal Error: Reversing this progression (such as starting with dark yellow and moving to light blue) appears unnatural, contradicts human cognitive models of illumination, and fails as a sequential scale!

Diverging Color Scales & The Neutral Midpoint

When data values deviate in two opposite directions relative to a critical baseline or neutral reference point, a sequential scale is inadequate. We must use a diverging color scale.

Diverging Color Scale: Structural Requirements

A diverging color scale consists of two sequential scales stitched together at a common neutral midpoint (typically represented by a light, desaturated color like white, light gray, or pale yellow).

The Balance Rule: The progression from light center to dark extremes must be visually symmetrical in both directions. If one side darkens faster than the other, viewers will perceive values on that side as having greater deviation or magnitude than equivalent values on the other side.

Texas Counties: When Percentages Require a Diverging Scale

Percentages are mathematically always positive ($0\%$ to $100\%$). One might naively assume they require a sequential scale. However, Wilke demonstrates in Figure 4-6 that for the percentage of people identifying as white in Texas counties, a diverging scale is mandatory:

  • The Midpoint: $50\%$ represents the demographic tipping point.
  • Above $50\%$: Whites are in the majority (encoded in shades of blue).
  • Below $50\%$: Whites are in the minority (encoded in shades of red/brown).
  • At $50\%$: Equal proportions (encoded as pale neutral white).

Conclusion: A diverging scale is justified whenever a specific reference point has profound qualitative or domain significance, regardless of whether numbers are negative!

Missing Data Convention in Choropleths

In Figure 4-4 (Median Income in Texas Counties), Loving County in West Texas had no data available. Wilke explicitly colors Loving County in neutral light gray. Exam Rule: Missing or undefined values must always be rendered in an unambiguous neutral tone (gray) that is not part of the active color scale.

Practice Questions (Topic 2)

Q1: Why does a multi-hue scale progressing from dark yellow to light blue fail as a sequential scale?

Answer: It violates the natural light gradient law. Natural illumination transitions from dark cool tones to bright yellow/white; reversing it appears unnatural and hinders monotonic lightness perception.

Q2: What is the structural definition of a diverging color scale, and why must it be balanced?

Answer: It is two sequential scales stitched at a common light midpoint. It must be balanced so that equal numerical deviations above and below the midpoint produce equal perceived visual magnitude.

Q3: Can a variable that contains only positive numbers (like percentages from 0 to 100) use a diverging scale?

Answer: Yes. If a specific internal value (such as 50% majority/minority or 100% budget baseline) represents a critical qualitative dividing line, a diverging scale is justified.

3Color as a Tool to Highlight: Accent Scales & Contrast Traps

Guiding visual attention, the three methods to derive accent palettes, and the baseline desaturation remedy.

In exploratory analysis, every data point is equal. But in explanatory communication, specific categories or data points carry the core message of your narrative. We use color to highlight those focal elements so they pop out instantaneously.

Accent Color Scales

An accent color scale pairs a set of subdued, low-saturation baseline colors with a matching set of strong, vivid, darker, or highly saturated accent colors.

Three Ways to Derive Accent Color Scales

Wilke outlines three distinct engineering approaches to create effective accent scales:

  1. Scale Modification: Take an existing qualitative scale (like Okabe-Ito), lighten and desaturate the baseline categories, and darken/saturate the focal categories.
  2. Grayscale Pairing: Map all non-focal baseline categories to neutral gray tones, and assign vivid saturated colors exclusively to the focal categories.
  3. Pre-designed Accent Scales: Use established cartographic or design palettes specifically built for highlighting (such as ColorBrewer Accent).

Case Study: Highlighting Neighboring States (Texas vs. Louisiana)

Demonstrating how identical population data supports different narratives through strategic accent coloring.

The Baseline Competition Trap & Wilke's Universal Remedy

A very common design error is using baseline colors that are still moderately saturated. When baseline elements are colorful, they compete with the accent color for the viewer's attention, diluting the visual hierarchy.

Wilke's Remedy: When in doubt, remove all color completely from all background and baseline elements. Turn everything into shades of gray or muted slate, and reserve color exclusively for the highlighted data! (Demonstrated in Figure 4-9 with male professional athlete body types, highlighting track athletes).

Practice Questions (Topic 3)

Q1: What is the primary visual failure when designing accent color scales?

Answer: Making baseline colors too saturated, causing them to compete for the reader's attention against the intended accent highlights.

Q2: What is Wilke's "easy remedy" when baseline colors distract from the highlight?

Answer: Remove all color from every element in the figure except the specific highlighted categories or data points (full grayscale baseline).

Q3: In the comparison between Texas and Louisiana population growth, what insight was unlocked by accent coloring?

Answer: Even though both are immediate southern neighbors, Texas was the 5th fastest growing US state while Louisiana was the 3rd slowest.

4The Principle of Proportional Ink: Linear Axes & Baseline Laws

Bergstrom & West's law, the internal inconsistency of truncated axes, and the Facebook stock price collapse myth.

In visual displays, graphical elements occupy physical space on the screen or paper. When graphical elements have a defined visual extent—such as the height of a bar, the area of a polygon, or the shading under a curve—our visual cortex automatically interprets that physical area as a magnitude.

The Principle of Proportional Ink (Bergstrom & West, 2016)

"When a shaded region is used to represent a numerical value, the area of that shaded region should be directly proportional to the corresponding value."

What is "Ink"? In visualization literature, "ink" refers to any mark that deviates from the blank background (lines, dots, shaded areas, text). In Chapter 17, the primary focus is on shaded regions and bars.

The Dual Encoding & Internal Inconsistency on Linear Axes

In a standard bar plot along a linear axis, the data value is encoded in two distinct ways:

  1. The location of the bar endpoint along the axis.
  2. The physical length and surface area of the bar.

If the bar begins at $0$, both encodings are perfectly aligned. But if the bar begins at an arbitrary non-zero baseline (e.g., $y = \$50,000$), the figure becomes internally inconsistent: the endpoint shows the actual value, but the area represents the difference above the arbitrary cutoff!

The Human Perception Flaw

Human perception is hardwired to evaluate the overall size and length of the bar, NOT the tiny numeric coordinate of its endpoint relative to the axis tick marks. A truncated bar chart visually lies to the viewer's brain!

Case Study: Median Income in Hawaii Counties (Truncated vs. Zero Baseline)

Figure 17-1 vs. 17-2 in Claus O. Wilke. Truncating the y-axis at $50k exaggerates income differences by over 500%.

Truncated Baseline ($50,000) Misleading (Bad)

Honolulu appears 8 times wealthier than Hawaii County, when the true difference is merely $75k vs $52k (~1.4x).

Zero Baseline ($0) Accurate (Good)

Bars accurately convey comparable standards of living across counties. Bar length is strictly proportional to income.

The Absolute Inviolable Rule of Linear Bar Charts

Bars on a linear scale MUST ALWAYS start at 0.

There are no exceptions. If your bars do not start at zero, you are actively violating the Principle of Proportional Ink.

The Shaded Area Under Time Series (The Facebook Stock Example)

The proportional ink trap applies equally to shaded line charts (area charts). In Figure 17-3, Wilke examines a financial chart of Facebook (FB) stock price from Oct 2016 to Jan 2017:

  • The Deception: The y-axis starts at $\$110$ instead of $\$0$, and the area under the price line is filled with solid shading. Around Nov 1, 2016, the stock dropped from $\$133$ to $\$118$. Because the shading stops at $\$110$, the shaded area shrunk by almost $70\%$, conveying the visual illusion of a catastrophic company collapse!
  • The Reality (Figure 17-4): Plotted on a true $\$0$ to $\$150$ scale, the price decline was moderate and normal relative to the total value of the company.
  • Why Shading Exacerbates the Error: Shading fills the space between the curve and the axis. The eye perceives the height of the ink column as the stock price. If the axis is truncated, the ink represents only the difference from an arbitrary baseline.

How to Legally Show Small Changes & Differences

Does the zero-baseline rule mean we can never use bars or shading to highlight subtle differences? No! Wilke provides two legitimate techniques:

Valid Techniques for Small Magnitudes
  • Plot the Difference Directly (Starting at 0): Instead of plotting total income from $50k, plot the change in median income from 2010 to 2015 starting at $0$ (Figure 17-5). Positive growth bars extend upwards; negative growth (Hawaii County) extends downwards below $0$. Proportional ink is preserved!
  • Shade Relative to a Peak Baseline: Instead of shading Facebook's price down to an arbitrary floor, shade the drop downward from its temporary high ($133$) (Figure 17-6). The shaded area now legitimately measures the exact depth of the price decline.
  • Switch from Bars to Points (Dots): A dot encodes data strictly by its position along the axis. A dot has no length or fill area. Therefore, a dot plot (Cleveland plot) on a truncated axis does NOT violate proportional ink!

Practice Questions (Topic 4)

Q1: Formulate Bergstrom & West's Principle of Proportional Ink in exact textbook wording.

Answer: "When a shaded region is used to represent a numerical value, the area of that shaded region should be directly proportional to the corresponding value."

Q2: Why does plotting dots (scatter/dot plots) on a truncated axis not violate proportional ink, whereas bars do?

Answer: Dots encode data solely through coordinate position along an axis, not through physical length or area. Bars encode data through visual extent/area, which becomes inconsistent if truncated.

Q3: How can a visualization legitimately use bars to show modest year-over-year income changes without violating proportional ink?

Answer: By explicitly defining the variable as the numerical change ($\Delta y$) and starting the axis at $0$, with positive changes extending upward and negative changes extending downward.

5Proportional Ink on Logarithmic Axes & Direct Area Perception

The fatal flaw of bars on log scales, amounts vs. ratios, the Cleveland dot plot alternative, and pie chart area limitations.

When visualizing data along a logarithmic axis, spatial distance is non-linear: each constant step represents multiplication by a factor (e.g., $10\times$ or $2\times$). This creates a unique mathematical dilemma for the Principle of Proportional Ink.

The Logarithmic Axis Proportional Ink Dilemma

On a log scale, the physical area of a bar is proportional to the logarithm of the data value ($\log x$), NOT to the value $x$ itself. Therefore:

  • Bars on a log scale satisfy proportional ink in log-transformed space.
  • Bars on a log scale violate proportional ink in linear real-world space.

To resolve this conflict, Wilke states the decisive test: Are you visualizing amounts or ratios?

Amounts vs. Ratios on a Logarithmic Scale

Consider the Gross Domestic Products (GDPs) of countries in Oceania in 2007, which ranged from less than $\$1$ billion to over $\$300$ billion (Figures 17-7 and 17-8):

  • Why linear bars fail: Australia and New Zealand completely dwarf all other island nations; Tuvalu and Kiribati become invisible 0-pixel slivers.
  • Why log bars fail for amounts: On a logarithmic axis, $0$ is located at $-\infty$. Where does the bar start? If you start bars at $\$0.3$ billion, the bar length represents excess over $0.3\text{B}$. If you push the origin to $10^{-9}$ billion, all bars become enormous. Any bar origin on a log scale for amounts is 100% arbitrary and meaningless!
Remedy for Amounts on a Log Scale: Cleveland Dot Plots

To visualize amounts spanning multiple orders of magnitude, never use bars. Place a dot at each country's GDP value along the log axis (Figure 17-9).

Wilke's Critical Label Placement Rule: Place the country names directly next to the dots rather than along the y-axis margin! If names are on the margin, the white space between the label and the dot creates the illusion of a horizontal bar length.

When ARE Bars Allowed on a Log Scale? (Ratios!)

Bars on a log scale are not just permissible—they are superior to linear bars—when visualizing ratios relative to a reference standard (Figure 17-10).

The Logarithmic Ratio Baseline Law

The natural mathematical neutral midpoint of a ratio is $1$ (where numerator equals denominator: $A/B = 1$). On a logarithmic axis, $\log(1) = 0$.

  • Bars representing ratios $> 1$ extend to the right (multiples).
  • Bars representing ratios $< 1$ extend to the left (fractions).

Exam Golden Rule: Bars on a linear scale represent amounts and MUST start at 0. Bars on a log scale represent ratios and MUST start at 1.

Property Linear Scale Bars Logarithmic Scale Bars
Valid Data Type Amounts (counts, revenue, weights) Ratios (relative to reference standard)
Mandatory Baseline Origin Must start at 0 Must start at 1 (where $\log(1) = 0$)
Opposite Direction Meaning Values below 0 (negative quantities) Ratios below 1 (fractions: $1/2, 1/4, 1/16$)
Fatal Anti-Pattern Truncating baseline (e.g., starting at 50) Using bars for absolute amounts (origin at $-\infty$)
Recommended Alternative Dot plot or slopegraph if showing small diffs Cleveland Dot Plot (dots with direct labels)

Direct Area Visualizations: Pie Charts & Treemaps

Unlike bar charts (where area is secondary to linear coordinate position), pie charts and treemaps map numerical values directly onto 2D surface areas.

Direct Area Perception & Perceptual Inaccuracy

Do pie charts violate proportional ink? Technically, no! In a 2D pie chart, the angle of each wedge and the surface area of each slice are strictly proportional to the data value.

The Perceptual Problem: Human visual psychology judges 1D linear lengths and distances with far higher precision than 2D areas or angles.

Rhode Island Counties: Pie Chart vs. Bar Plot (Figures 17-11 to 17-13)

Wilke demonstrates county population across Rhode Island using three charts:

  • Pie Chart (17-11): Providence County dominates, but estimating whether Kent is twice Washington County or three times Bristol County is mentally exhausting and imprecise.
  • Bar Plot (17-12): The length difference between Providence and other counties is instantly quantifiable. Distances along a common scale leverage human perceptual strengths.
  • Treemap (17-13): A square version of a pie chart. Rectangular areas with different aspect ratios (tall/skinny vs short/wide) are notoriously difficult for human eyes to compare accurately.

Practice Questions (Topic 5)

Q1: Why is it fundamentally impossible to draw legitimate bar charts of absolute amounts on a logarithmic scale?

Answer: On a logarithmic axis, $0$ lies infinitely far away at $-\infty$. Therefore, any bar origin selected is completely arbitrary and makes bar lengths meaningless.

Q2: What is the mandatory starting baseline for bars drawn on a logarithmic scale, and what do those bars represent?

Answer: Bars on a log scale must start at 1, and they represent ratios relative to a baseline reference.

Q3: Do pie charts violate the Principle of Proportional Ink? If not, why are bar plots usually preferred?

Answer: No, pie charts satisfy proportional ink (wedge area is proportional to value). Bar plots are preferred because human perception judges 1D linear lengths far more accurately than 2D areas or angles.

Chapter 4 & 17 High-Yield Takeaways
  • 3 Color Uses: Distinguish (qualitative nominal), Represent values (quantitative sequential/diverging), Highlight (accent).
  • Qualitative Rule: Finite distinct hues, equal visual weight, no perceived order. Standard: Okabe-Ito (colorblind safe).
  • Sequential Gradient Rule: Must vary perceptually uniformly. Multi-hue scales must follow natural gradients (dark cool/warm → light yellow). Reversing is fatal.
  • Diverging Rule: Two sequential scales stitched at neutral light midpoint. Must be balanced symmetrically. Midpoint must have domain meaning (e.g., $50\%$).
  • Accent Remedy: If baseline colors compete with accent highlights, strip all color from baseline (pure grayscale background).
  • Proportional Ink Definition: Shaded region area must be directly proportional to represented numerical data value (Bergstrom & West).
  • Linear Bar Law: Bars on linear axes must always start at 0. Truncating creates internal inconsistency between endpoint and bar length.
  • Log Bar Law: Never use bars for amounts on log scale (origin at $-\infty$; use Cleveland dot plots). Bars on log scale must start at 1 and represent ratios.
  • Direct Area Law: Pie charts and treemaps satisfy proportional ink, but human eyes judge linear lengths far more accurately than 2D areas.
