Module 1 — Introduction to Veridical Data Science

1What is Veridical Data Science?

Truthful, trustworthy, real-world data analysis — not just running algorithms

The word veridical means truthful or coinciding with reality. Veridical Data Science (VDS) is not just a toolbox of algorithms — it spans the entire data science life cycle, demanding that every result be trustworthy and relevant to the real-world problem at hand.

Definition — Veridical Data Science
The practice of conducting data analysis while making human judgment calls and using domain knowledge to extract and communicate useful and trustworthy information from data to solve a real-world domain problem. (Bin Yu & Rebecca L. Barter, MIT Press)
Definition — Algorithm
A set of instructions telling a computer to perform a task — from computing a mean, to producing a scatterplot, to training a neural network. Every algorithm is a mental construct created by humans. Even the humble mean is an algorithm.

Data Science = 3 Intersecting Pillars

Pillar What it covers Without it…
💻 Computer Science Code, algorithms, scalability Can't process large data
📐 Statistics / Math Signal vs. noise, uncertainty Meaningless outputs
🔬 Domain Knowledge Context, interpretation, ethics Misleading conclusions
Key Insight
Data science is NOT a subfield of statistics or computer science. It is the integration of all three pillars applied to real-world domain problems.

Key Takeaways

  • Veridical = truthful; the goal is trustworthy results, not just any results
  • Every algorithm — even computing a mean — is a human-made mental construct
  • DS requires CS + Statistics + Domain Knowledge simultaneously
  • VDS spans the full life cycle: problem → data → analysis → communication
Practice Q: What does "veridical" mean and why does it matter for data science?

Answer: Veridical means "truthful" or "coinciding with reality." It matters because DS results drive real-world decisions (medical, financial, policy). If results are not genuinely trustworthy — just fitting artifacts in noisy data — those decisions will be wrong. VDS provides a framework to actively demonstrate trustworthiness rather than assuming it.

Practice Q: True or False — "Algorithm" in VDS refers only to machine learning models.

Answer: FALSE. The term is used broadly. Computing a mean is an algorithm. Making a scatterplot is an algorithm. Any computational procedure that extracts a pattern from data counts. This broad definition reminds us that every analysis choice — not just complex ML — requires scrutiny.

Hard Q: Why can't we simply automate the data science process end-to-end with AI?

Answer: Because key steps require human judgment and domain knowledge. Automated systems don't know that "99" in a medical survey means "declined to answer," or that a data collection flaw makes certain demographics underrepresented. They can't judge whether results are ethically sound or whether the framing of the question is appropriate. Critical thinking about reality, data, and algorithms cannot be fully automated.

2The Three Realms

Reality → Data → Algorithms: the three worlds every data scientist inhabits

One of VDS's most powerful insights: every data science project operates across three distinct realms. Gaps between these realms are where errors, misleading results, and failures hide.

🌍

Reality

The actual world. We can never fully observe it — only measure it.

→
📊

Data (Approx. of Reality)

A crude, partial, time-stamped snapshot of measurements.

→
🧠

Mental Constructs

Our analyses and algorithms — human-designed models of reality.

The Two Critical Gaps
Gap 1 — Data vs Reality: Does our dataset adequately represent reality? Biased sampling, measurement errors, and outdated data all widen this gap.

Gap 2 — Algorithm vs Reality: Do our algorithms capture real patterns, or are they fitting noise/artifacts? Will conclusions hold on future data from the same real-world system?
Example — Hospital Readmission

A model is trained on 500 American patients to predict hospital readmission. It achieves 90% accuracy.

  • Reality: True biological/social risk factors for all patients globally
  • Data: 500 Americans in 2022 — a tiny, time-limited, region-limited snapshot
  • Algorithm: Logistic regression fitted to those 500 patients
  • Risk: Applied to European hospitals, it may fail — the data poorly approximated broader reality
Common Mistake
"If my model fits the data well, I'm done." Wrong! The model might be fitting artifacts of the specific data collection, not real-world patterns. Trustworthiness requires evaluating on future, unseen data that better represents reality.

Key Takeaways

  • 3 realms: Reality → Data (approximation) → Algorithms (mental constructs)
  • Data is imperfect, partial, time-stamped — never a perfect mirror of reality
  • Algorithms are assumptions-in-code, not objective truth
  • Trustworthy DS = tight alignment across ALL three realms for both current AND future data
Practice Q: Name and describe the three realms of veridical data science.

Answer: (1) Reality — the actual real-world system being studied; we never directly observe it. (2) Approximation of Reality (Data Realm) — our dataset, an imprecise partial snapshot of reality taken at a moment in time. (3) Mental Constructs (Algorithmic Realm) — our analyses and algorithms, designed based on our assumptions about how reality works.

Hard Q: Why is the gap between the data realm and reality unavoidable?

Answer: Two fundamental reasons: (1) Measurement imprecision — instruments and human recording introduce errors; no measurement perfectly captures the true real-world quantity. (2) Sampling and temporal limitations — data is collected from a finite subset of the population at a specific moment. This cannot represent all conditions, all people, all future states. Reality continues changing; datasets stay frozen.

3Data, Algorithms & Decision Making

How imperfect inputs produce real-world consequences

The ultimate goal of DS: use data-driven insights to make real-world decisions. But those decisions are only as trustworthy as the weakest link in the chain: Reality → Data → Algorithm → Decision.

Definition — Current vs Future Data
Current Data: The dataset you use to conduct analysis and train your algorithm.

Future Data: The real-world data to which you will apply your conclusions. This is what actually matters — your algorithm needs to work on future data, not just memorize the current dataset.
Definition — Uncertainty
The extent to which a data-driven result could have plausibly ended up being different, arising from: different data samples, different cleaning choices, different algorithm selections, different parameters.

Sources of Uncertainty

Source Example Perturbation
Data Collection Different 500 patients → different dataset
Data Cleaning Drop missing vs. impute → different cleaned data
Algorithm Choice Logistic Regression vs. Random Forest → different predictions
Hyperparameters Different tree depth → different model
Time Model from 2022, applied in 2025 — reality shifted
The Core VDS Question
How stable are your results across these sources of uncertainty? If conclusions flip when you change one cleaning step, how much should you trust them?

Key Takeaways

  • Current data = what you have; Future data = where your results must work
  • Uncertainty is inherent — in data collection, cleaning, analysis choices, and time
  • High-stakes decisions require stable results across multiple sources of uncertainty
Practice Q: Distinguish current data from future data. Why does the distinction matter?

Answer: Current data is used to build and train the model. Future data is where the model will actually be applied in the real world. The distinction matters because a model can perform perfectly on current data by memorizing patterns specific to that dataset (overfitting), yet completely fail on future data that differs even slightly. The goal of DS is always to make good decisions on future data — so evaluating performance on future-like held-out data is essential.

Practice Q: List 3 sources of uncertainty in a data science project.

Answer: (1) Data collection uncertainty — different samples from the same population give different datasets. (2) Data cleaning/preprocessing uncertainty — different judgment calls (e.g., missing value treatment) produce different cleaned datasets. (3) Algorithm/model uncertainty — different algorithms or different hyperparameters produce different results. Additional valid sources: measurement error, sampling bias, temporal drift.

4Critical Thinking & Cognitive Biases

The human judgment layer that no algorithm can replace

Critical thinking must be applied at three levels in every data science project. It is your defense against garbage-in/garbage-out, misleading analyses, and unethical outcomes.

Level 1 — Question the Domain Problem

Slide Example — Two Framings
Narrow (Dangerous) Better (Critical)
"Which website features make customers less likely to cancel?" "Why do customers cancel our subscription?"
Assumes the website is the only problem. Misses root causes. Opens inquiry to all causes: pricing, product, competition, UX, support.

Ask: Is this the right question? Is it ethical? Are we solving the actual problem?

Level 2 — Question the Data

  • 📍 Source: Where did it come from? Who collected it and why?
  • ✅ Trustworthy? Could there be bias, errors, or conflicts of interest?
  • 🎯 Relevant? Does it match the question AND the future context where results will be applied?
  • 📖 Values: What do specific values actually mean? (e.g., "99" = "declined to answer")

Level 3 — Question the Analysis & Results

  • 🌍 Do results make sense in reality? Do they pass the common-sense test?
  • ⚙️ What assumptions does the algorithm make? Are they valid for this data?
  • ❓ Do the results actually answer the original question?
  • 📢 Are results being communicated accurately, without misleading visualizations?

Cognitive Biases — The Silent Killers

Definition — Confirmation Bias
Failing to critically scrutinize results because they match what you expected. You stop questioning because the output "feels right" — even if it's wrong. Leads to trusting a bad model that simply confirms prior beliefs.
Definition — Desirability Bias
Steering analysis toward the results that other people (or popular opinion) want to see. Often driven by business or social pressure. The analyst produces "pleasing" results rather than truthful ones.
Common Mistake — p-Hacking / Data Snooping
Running many analyses until one gives a "significant" result, then reporting only that one. This inflates false-positive rates. Results produced this way will not replicate. Document all analyses you ran, not just the best-looking one.

Key Takeaways

  • Critical thinking operates at 3 levels: domain problem → data → analysis
  • Confirmation bias: accepting results because they match prior expectations
  • Desirability bias: producing results to please stakeholders, not find truth
  • Always ask: Is this the right question? Is the data trustworthy and relevant?
  • Case study: 2019 Australian bushfire "1 billion animals" = a modeled estimate dependent on assumptions — not a direct count
Practice Q: How do confirmation bias and desirability bias differ?

Answer: Confirmation bias is internal — you stop scrutinizing because results align with your own prior beliefs. Desirability bias is external — you steer the analysis to produce what others want to see (bosses, clients, popular opinion). Both lead to untrustworthy results. Key: confirmation bias = your expectations; desirability bias = others' expectations.

Hard Q: A dataset has a value of "99" for patient age. What critical thinking steps should you take?

Answer: (1) Is 99 a plausible age? Could be real, but unlikely in bulk. (2) Check the data documentation/codebook — "99" often means "missing/declined." (3) Ask the data collector if possible. (4) Check surrounding fields for consistency — if other fields suggest the patient is ~30, "99" is clearly a code. (5) Document your judgment call: decide whether to treat as missing (NA), exclude the record, or impute, and record why. (6) Test stability: does your conclusion change if you handle these differently?

5The PCS Framework

Predictability + Computability + Stability = the systematic path to trustworthy results

Critical thinking tells us what to question. The PCS Framework gives a systematic method to demonstrate trustworthiness computationally. It is the mathematical heart of veridical data science.

P

Predictability

Results hold in relevant future/external data — the "reality check."

C

Computability

Computations are clear, efficient, scalable, and reproducible.

S

Stability

Conclusions stay consistent under reasonable perturbations to data or methods.

Predictability — Deep Dive
Computationally demonstrating that results reemerge in relevant future or external data and align with domain knowledge.
How: Train/validation/test split. Train set builds model; validation set tunes it; test set (held out until the very end) gives the final honest evaluation of generalization. Or: collect genuinely new data from the real-world domain.
Computability — Deep Dive
Code is written so others (or future you) can reproduce every result exactly. Implies: version control (Git), documented functions, no random seeds left unset, clear variable names. Computations must also be feasible given time/resource constraints.
Stability — Deep Dive
Conclusions remain fairly consistent when realistic changes are made to: (1) the data (different sample, different cleaning), (2) judgment calls (different missing-value strategy), and (3) algorithm choices (different model, different hyperparameters). Sensitive results are untrustworthy.
Train / Validation / Test Split
All Data
├── Training Set (~65%)  → Fit the model
├── Validation Set (~20%) → Tune parameters, intermediate checks
└── Test Set (~15%)       → FINAL honest evaluation of predictability
                            Never peek until development is complete!

Rule: Peeking at the test set during development = data leakage = dishonest predictability evaluation.

Exam Hint
PCS letters: P = future data check, C = reproducible code, S = robust to perturbations. PCS is applied throughout the entire DSLC, not just at the modeling stage.

Key Takeaways

  • Predictability: Results generalize to future/external data (test set or new data)
  • Computability: Clear, reproducible, scalable code
  • Stability: Robust to reasonable perturbations in data & method choices
  • PCS = a framework for demonstrating trustworthiness, not assuming it
  • Test set must be held out and never used until final evaluation
Practice Q: Explain each letter of PCS with one sentence each.

P — Predictability: Results hold when evaluated on relevant future or external data (reality check). C — Computability: Computations are clear, efficient, scalable, and reproducible by others. S — Stability: Conclusions stay fairly consistent when realistic changes are made to data, judgment calls, or algorithm choices.

Practice Q: Why must the test set be kept hidden until the very end?

Answer: The test set simulates truly unseen future data. If you use it to tune the model, you introduce data leakage — the model is indirectly fitted to it, and your final accuracy metric is no longer an honest measure of how the model will perform on genuine future data. Predictability evaluation becomes meaningless.

Hard Q: Is cross-validating across 10 random splits sufficient to establish full stability? Explain.

Answer: No — it's only partial stability. Cross-validation tests stability to sampling variability within the current dataset. Full PCS stability also requires testing: (1) stability to data cleaning choices (e.g., different missing value strategies), (2) stability to preprocessing choices (normalization vs. standardization), and (3) stability to algorithm/model choices. A result should hold across all three perturbation types to be declared stable.

6Anscombe's Quartet

Why four "identical" datasets are completely different — and why you MUST visualize your data

The opening slide of this course presents Anscombe's Quartet — the most famous demonstration in statistics that summary numbers lie.

Four Datasets. Identical Statistics.

Statistic A B C D
Mean of X 9 9 9 9
Variance of X 11 11 11 11
Mean of Y 7.50 7.50 7.50 7.50
Variance of Y 4.125 4.125 4.125 4.125
Correlation (X,Y) 0.816 0.816 0.816 0.816

If you only read the numbers — you'd declare all four identical. They're not.

Four Completely Different Stories

Dataset A — Genuinely linear

Dataset B — Curved (non-linear)

Dataset C — Outlier-driven

Dataset D — Leverage point

The Lesson
Summary statistics are insufficient. Means, variances, and correlations can be identical for datasets with radically different shapes, requiring completely different models and interpretations. Always plot your data first. This is the foundational motivation for EDA.
Common Mistake
Reporting "there is a strong linear correlation (r = 0.816)" without ever visualizing the data. Dataset B has r = 0.816 but is clearly non-linear — a linear model would give wrong predictions. Dataset D has r = 0.816 but it's entirely driven by one artificial leverage point.

Key Takeaways

  • 4 datasets → identical statistics → completely different structures
  • A: genuine linear; B: curved (wrong model); C: one outlier skews regression; D: artificial leverage
  • Correlation ≠ shape; statistics ≠ understanding
  • Always visualize data before interpreting any summary statistic
  • This is the #1 motivation for Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
Practice Q: What is the key lesson of Anscombe's Quartet?

Answer: That summary statistics alone (mean, variance, correlation) are insufficient to understand a dataset. Four datasets with identical summary statistics can have completely different underlying structures — a genuine linear relationship, a curved relationship, an outlier-driven spurious correlation, and a leverage-point artifact. This demonstrates why data visualization is mandatory, not optional, in any honest analysis.

Practice Q: What makes Dataset D particularly deceptive?

Answer: Dataset D has all data points clustered at one x-value (creating no real x-variation), plus one outlier at a very high x-value. This single point has extreme "leverage" — it creates the apparent linear correlation almost entirely by itself. Remove that one point and the correlation vanishes. Without a plot, you'd think you have a strongly correlated linear relationship, when in fact the correlation is an artifact of one anomalous data point.

🚩 Exam Traps — Don't Fall For These

Trap 1
"Data science is a subfield of statistics." → FALSE. It is the integration of CS, statistics, and domain knowledge.
Trap 2
"A model with 98% training accuracy is trustworthy." → FALSE. Could be overfitting. Need to evaluate on held-out future data.
Trap 3
"All four Anscombe datasets have different correlations." → FALSE. All have r ≈ 0.816. That's the entire point.
Trap 4
"Confirmation bias = wanting positive results." → PARTIALLY FALSE. It's about results matching your prior beliefs (positive or negative). Desirability bias = wanting results that please others.

🎯 Likely Quiz Questions (with Answers)

Q: What does P stand for in PCS, and how do you evaluate it?

A: P = Predictability. Evaluated by demonstrating results hold on relevant future or external data. Mechanically: split data into training/validation/test sets; use the test set (held out, never peeked at) for final evaluation. Or: collect new data from the target real-world domain and test there.

Q: An analyst's conclusion changes dramatically when they switch from removing missing values to imputing them. What does PCS say about this?

A: The conclusion lacks Stability (the S in PCS). It is highly sensitive to one data cleaning judgment call. Under VDS, this instability is itself an important finding — it means neither conclusion should be reported as trustworthy without further investigation. Both cleaning strategies should be documented, and domain knowledge should guide the choice. The sensitivity should be reported to stakeholders.

Q: True or False — VDS focuses only on the modeling/ML stage of a project.

A: FALSE. VDS spans the entire data science life cycle: from problem formulation, data collection, cleaning, EDA, and modeling, through evaluation and communication of results. The PCS framework is applied at every stage, not just modeling.

Q: A researcher reports a correlation of 0.81 between X and Y and concludes "there is a strong linear relationship." What is missing?

A: A visualization. As Anscombe's Quartet demonstrates, a correlation of ~0.816 can arise from: a genuine linear relationship (A), a curved non-linear relationship where linear is wrong (B), a nearly perfect linear dataset with one outlier distorting the regression (C), or an artificial correlation created by a single high-leverage point (D). Without a scatter plot, the analyst cannot distinguish between these four fundamentally different scenarios.

Chapter 1 — The Big Picture in 5 Points

  • VDS = Truthful DS operating across 3 realms: Reality → Data → Algorithms
  • The core challenge: imperfect data + imperfect algorithms → results that may not reflect reality
  • Critical thinking (at domain, data, and analysis levels) is the human defense layer
  • PCS = systematic framework: Predictability + Computability + Stability
  • Anscombe's Quartet: always plot your data — summary statistics cannot be trusted alone
