Visualizing Time Series & Trends

Textbook & Lecture References Claus O. Wilke: Fundamentals of Data Visualization, Chapter 13 (Time Series & Independent Variables) & Chapter 14 (Visualizing Trends) • Course Handout: Notes on Time Series and Trends (Local Regression, LOESS, Splines, Eager vs. Lazy Computation, Data Straightening & Conversions).

1Time Series Foundations & Line Graph Principles

Temporal ordering, points vs. lines, shaded area baselines, direct labeling, and dose–response curves (Wilke Ch. 13)

When visualizing quantitative variables against one another, standard scatterplots treat both axes symmetrically with no assumed sequence. However, when the independent variable is time (or a controlled, ordered experimental dose), the data acquires an inherent sequential structure. Every observation has an unambiguous predecessor and successor. Line graphs exist specifically to communicate and emphasize this temporal progression.

Time Series: A sequence of data points recorded at successive, ordered intervals of an independent variable (typically time $t$).
Line Graph: A chart that connects consecutive data points along an ordered independent axis with straight line segments to guide the human eye along the trajectory of change.
Intuitive one-liner: A line graph turns discrete snapshots of time into a continuous visual story.

1. The Visual Spectrum: Dots, Lines, and Guides to the Eye

How should discrete time points be rendered? Claus O. Wilke outlines four distinct visual treatments, each suited to specific data densities and analytical goals:

1. Dots Only (Scatterplot) Discrete Only

Plots raw observations without connecting segments. Emphasizes individual measurements, but forces the viewer to mentally connect neighbors. Poor for tracking rapid fluctuations.

2. Dots + Connecting Lines Guide to Eye

Explicitly highlights sequence while preserving exact measurement points. Ideal for sparse, irregularly spaced, or moderate-sized series where individual samples matter.

3. Lines Only (No Dots) Dense Data Best

Omits dots entirely to reduce visual clutter. Maximizes focus on macro-trends and curvature. Recommended when time points are densely spaced (e.g., daily stock prices or monthly submissions over years).

4. Filled Area Under Curve Strict Baseline

Shades the region between the line and the horizontal axis. Emphasizes cumulative volume or magnitude. Valid ONLY if the vertical axis begins at absolute zero.

Architectural Visual: The 4 Renderings of a Time Series & The Zero-Baseline Law

Comparison of representation modes for the bioRxiv monthly submission series and the visual hazard of truncated baselines.

The "Guide to the Eye" Philosophical Debate: Some purists object to line graphs because connecting segments represent unobserved, interpolated data. If measurements had been taken at midpoints, they would almost certainly not have landed squarely on the linear segment. Wilke resolves this dilemma: connecting lines are not claims of physical intermediate values; they are purely perceptual guides that exploit human Gestalt continuity to trace time's arrow. It is standard practice to state in captions: "Lines are meant as a guide to the eye."
Critical Exam Pitfall — Truncated Area Charts: In a regular line chart, truncating the vertical axis (e.g., showing Dow Jones between 24,000 and 26,000) is often acceptable to reveal small percentage variations. In an area chart, this is strictly forbidden. The human visual cortex automatically equates shaded pixel area with physical quantity. Truncating the axis causes an item with value 25,000 to look 10× larger than one with 24,100. Never shade under a curve unless the baseline is true zero.

2. Multiple Time Series: Spaghetti Plots vs. Direct Labeling

When tracking multiple categories over time (e.g., bioRxiv, arXiv q-bio, and PeerJ Preprints), plotting unadorned points yields a confusing jumble of overlapping marks (a spaghetti plot).

Strategy Implementation Cognitive Load Best Use Case
Separate Legend Different colors/glyphs linked to an external box High (Continuous saccadic eye jumping between key & curves) More than 5–6 series where end-of-line space is strictly constrained.
Direct Labeling Text label placed immediately adjacent to the terminus of each line Lowest (Direct foveal reading without translation) Preferred gold standard for 2–5 distinct series. Eliminates glyph variety requirements.
Small Multiples (Faceting) Separate synchronized subpanels sharing identical scales Low (Isolates trajectories, avoids overlap) Many overlapping series with heavy intersection or crossing trajectories.

3. Extending Beyond Time: Dose–Response Curves

Line graphs are fundamentally applicable whenever the independent variable imposes an order on the data, even if that variable is not chronological time. A prime scientific example is the dose–response curve.

Case Study: Oat Yield vs. Manure Fertilization (Yates 1935):

In agricultural experiments, researchers measured oat yield (bushels/acre) across three distinct oat varieties as a function of nitrogen fertilization (manure application in hundredweight [cwt] per acre; $1 ext{ cwt} = 112 ext{ lbs} pprox 50.8 ext{ kg}$).

  1. Ordered Independent Variable: Manure dose ($x$: 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 cwt/acre) is controlled and strictly monotonically increasing.
  2. Visual Insight: Connecting the mean yield per dose with lines immediately reveals that all three oat varieties share an identical diminishing-returns slope, but differ in baseline intercept (Variety 1 has naturally superior yield in unfertilized soil).
  3. Takeaway: Line plots expose whether treatments alter response trajectories or merely shift baseline performance.

Self-Assessment Practice Questions

Question 1 (Easy): Under what specific condition is shading the area underneath a line graph statistically valid?
Answer: Only when the vertical ($y$) axis begins at absolute zero.

Explanation: The visual area of a polygon communicates cumulative magnitude. If the $y$-axis starts at a non-zero number, the geometric area is artificially truncated and does not scale proportionally with the actual data values, creating severe visual distortion.

Question 2 (Medium): Why does Wilke strongly recommend direct labeling over external legends for multiple line series?
Answer: To eliminate cognitive load caused by saccadic eye movement and working memory buffering.

Explanation: External legends force the reader's eyes to constantly scan back and forth between the plot area and the legend key, matching colors or symbols. Placing labels directly at the end of each line allows immediate comprehension and removes the need for contrasting symbol shapes.

Question 3 (Hard): When is it preferable to omit the data dots entirely in a time-series line graph?
Answer: When observations are sampled densely over time.

Explanation: With dense sampling (e.g., hundreds or thousands of daily stock quotes or monthly submissions), displaying individual dots adds visual noise and creates an inky overplotted smudge. Omitting dots highlights the underlying trajectory and curvature without sacrificing meaningful resolution.

Topic 1 Key Takeaways

  • Order Imposes Linearity: Time and ordered experimental doses turn unordered point clouds into chronological paths where lines guide visual continuity.
  • Dots vs. Lines: Sparse data benefits from dots + lines; dense data demands lines only. Connecting lines are guides to the eye, not verified data points.
  • Zero-Baseline Law: Area charts must anchor at $y=0$. Truncated area charts mislead the human perception of volume.
  • Direct Labeling: Always prefer labeling lines at their endpoints over external legends for 2–5 distinct series.

2Multiple Response Variables & Connected Scatterplots

Dual time series, phase portraits, cyclical spirals, readability trade-offs, and PCA trajectories (Wilke Ch. 13)

In macroeconomic and multi-sensor systems, analysts frequently monitor two or more continuous response variables evolving simultaneously over time (such as the 12-month percent change in house prices alongside the national unemployment rate). Visualizing their joint dynamic relationship presents a profound design choice: stack separate line graphs, or fuse them into a connected scatterplot.

Connected Scatterplot (Phase Portrait): A bivariate visualization that maps two response variables to Cartesian coordinates ($x$ and $y$) and connects consecutive time points in chronological order with directed line segments.
Phase Space: A conceptual space where all possible states of a system are represented, with each instantaneous state corresponding to a unique coordinate point.
Intuitive one-liner: Instead of showing time on an axis, time becomes the path traveled through the coordinate space.

1. Stacked Line Graphs vs. Connected Scatterplots

When two variables are shown as separate stacked line plots sharing a synchronized horizontal time axis, reading individual values is straightforward. However, identifying cross-variable correlations requires awkward, back-and-forth cognitive slope comparisons.

Architectural Visual: Stacked Line Graphs vs. Connected Scatterplot Phase Dynamics

Macroeconomic case study: House price index 12-month change (%) vs. Unemployment rate (%) over time.

2. Reading Geometric Vectors in Connected Scatterplots

Connected scatterplots transform time-series interpretation from chronological slopes into spatial vector directions:

Vector Trajectory Mathematical Relationship Physical / Economic Meaning
Lower-Left to Upper-Right ($ earrow$) Positive Correlation ($\Delta y / \Delta x > 0$) Both variables expand or contract concurrently.
Upper-Left to Lower-Right ($\searrow$) Negative / Anticorrelation ($\Delta y / \Delta x < 0$) Trade-off state: as variable $x$ expands, variable $y$ contracts.
Closed Circles / Spirals ($\circlearrowleft$ or $\circlearrowright$) Cyclical / Oscillatory Dynamic (Phase Shift) One variable leads the other in an out-of-phase feedback loop (e.g., boom $ o$ bust $ o$ recovery).
The "Meaningless Scribble" Failure Mode: If an author creates a connected scatterplot without directional indicators (arrowheads or temporal color gradients) and without date landmark labels, the chart completely degenerates into an illegible tangle of spaghetti (Wilke Fig 13-11). The viewer has no way of knowing where the trajectory began, which way time is flowing, or how quickly transitions occurred.

3. Cognitive Trade-offs & Empirical Findings

In an influential perceptual study, researchers tested reader comprehension between dual line graphs and connected scatterplots:

Haroz, Kosara, and Franconeri (2016) Findings:
  • Comprehension Cost: Readers are significantly more prone to confusing chronological order and misjudging raw correlation in connected scatterplots compared to dual line graphs. Line graphs remain cognitively simpler.
  • Engagement Benefit: Connected scatterplots generate markedly higher user engagement, captivate reader curiosity, and make narrative data exploration memorable.
  • Golden Rule: Use line graphs for quick, effortless reference. Use connected scatterplots when presenting cyclical, looping relationships where narrative engagement is paramount.

4. High-Dimensional Time Series in PCA Space

Although Cartesian space is limited to two dimensions ($x$ and $y$), connected scatterplots can visualize systems with hundreds of concurrent time series by performing Principal Components Analysis (PCA) first.

Case Study: 100+ Macroeconomic Indicators (St. Louis Fed):

The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis maintains over 100 monthly economic metrics (employment, GDP, inflation, bond yields, housing starts).

  1. PC1 (System Scale): In macroeconomic PCA, the first principal component (PC1) almost always captures overall economic expansion. In a plot of PC2 vs. PC1, time moves almost linearly from left to right.
  2. Recessions in PC2: Recessions correspond to sudden vertical plunges in PC2 (March 1991, November 2001, June 2009).
  3. The Clockwise Spiral in PC2 vs. PC3: When plotting PC2 against PC3, the economic path forms a distinct clockwise spiral. Recessions drop precipitously along PC2, while recoveries correspond to transitions along PC3, visually cementing the cyclical clockwork of macroeconomic dynamics.

Self-Assessment Practice Questions

Question 1 (Easy): In a connected scatterplot, what does a trajectory moving from the upper-left to the lower-right signify?
Answer: An anticorrelated (negative) relationship between the two variables during that time period.

Explanation: Moving from upper-left to lower-right means that the $y$-axis variable is decreasing while the $x$-axis variable is increasing ($\Delta y < 0, \Delta x > 0$), which is the geometric signature of negative correlation.

Question 2 (Medium): What two mandatory visual elements must be present to prevent a connected scatterplot from becoming a "meaningless scribble"?
Answer: 1) Clear directional cues (such as arrowheads or continuous light-to-dark color shading), and 2) Chronological time markers (labeled key years or milestone dates).

Explanation: Because time is mapped along a winding path rather than a straight Cartesian axis, viewers cannot deduce the flow of time or the speed of transitions without explicit directional markers and date tags.

Question 3 (Hard): How does Principal Components Analysis (PCA) enable connected scatterplots to visualize high-dimensional time series?
Answer: By reducing $N$ dimensional time-series variables to 2 or 3 orthogonal principal components, allowing joint trajectory plotting in reduced phase space.

Explanation: PCA condenses high-dimensional variance into orthogonal components (e.g., PC1 measuring overall economic size, PC2 and PC3 capturing cyclical shocks and recoveries). Plotting PC2 vs. PC3 creates a phase portrait revealing complex multidimensional cycles (like economic recessions).

Topic 2 Key Takeaways

  • Phase Portraits: Connected scatterplots map two dependent variables on $x$ and $y$, turning time into a traveled trajectory.
  • Geometry of Loops: Diagonal lines show correlation; circular loops and spirals reveal cyclical feedback loops that remain obscured in separate line graphs.
  • Design Essentials: Never publish a connected scatterplot without directional flow (arrows/shading) and key date labels.
  • PCA Dimension Reduction: Reduces 100+ variables into 2D phase space trajectories (e.g., macroeconomic clockwise boom-bust cycles).

3Smoothing Techniques & Local Regression (LOESS)

Moving average limitations, the 6-step LOESS algorithm, tricube weighting, span tuning, and Savitzky–Golay connection (Wilke Ch. 14 & Handout)

When visualizing noisy time series (e.g., daily Dow Jones Industrial Average values) or scatterplots with large point clouds, the overarching trend is often obscured by high-frequency variance. Smoothing extracts the underlying deterministic signal while filtering out stochastic noise.

Moving Average (Rolling Mean): A naive smoothing technique where each data point is replaced by the arithmetic mean of $k$ neighboring observations inside a sliding window.
LOESS (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing): A non-parametric regression method that builds a smooth trend line point-by-point by fitting localized, weighted polynomials to subsets of the data.
LOWESS (Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing): The linear (degree-1) variant of local regression. Both terms are pronounced "LOH-ess".
Intuitive one-liner: Instead of forcing one rigid equation onto the entire dataset, LOESS fits thousands of tiny, localized lines and glues their centers together.

1. Why Moving Averages Fail: The Window Boundary Problem

While moving averages are simple to compute, they suffer from three fundamental mathematical defects:

1. Boundary Wiggles (Square Wave Weight) Defect

All points inside the window are weighted equally ($1/k$), while points outside have weight $0$. As an extreme outlier enters or exits the boundary of the window, the moving average produces an artificial, abrupt jerk or "wiggle" (Wilke Fig 14-2).

2. Boundary Data Truncation Defect

A centered $k$-point moving average cannot evaluate points within $k/2$ steps of the dataset edges. For a 100-day average, the first and last 50 days of data are completely discarded.

3. The Noise vs. Lag Dilemma Trade-off

A short window (e.g., 20 days) remains jagged and noisy. A long window (e.g., 100 days) oversmooths and severely lags behind sharp market turns and real inflections.

Architectural Visual: Moving Average Equal-Weighting vs. LOESS Tricube Weighting

2. The Exact 6-Step LOESS Algorithmic Mechanism

The LOESS algorithm executes the following rigorous sequence point-by-point across the domain:

  1. Target Selection: The algorithm selects a target $x$-value ($x_0$) along the horizontal axis where it seeks to estimate a smoothed value $\hat{y}_0$.
  2. Neighborhood Construction (Span $\alpha$): It defines a local neighborhood window of nearby data points. The window size is governed by the span parameter ($\alpha$). For example, if $\alpha = 0.3$, the algorithm selects the $30\%$ of total observations closest in distance to $x_0$.
  3. Distance Weight Assignment (Tricube Function): Every point $x_i$ inside the neighborhood is assigned a continuous weight $w_i$ based on its normalized distance $u_i = \frac{|x_i - x_0|}{\Delta(x_0)}$, where $\Delta(x_0)$ is the distance to the furthest point in the neighborhood:
    W(u) = (1 - |u|³)³   for |u| ≤ 1,   and W(u) = 0   for |u| > 1
    The closest point receives weight $\approx 1.0$, weights smoothly decrease to $0.0$ at the neighborhood boundary, and points outside receive strictly $0$.
  4. Weighted Least Squares (WLS) Regression: Using only points in the neighborhood and their assigned weights $w_i$, the algorithm fits a localized regression:
    • Degree 1 (Linear): Fits $\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ (Standard LOWESS).
    • Degree 2 (Quadratic): Fits $\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2$ (Standard LOESS; significantly superior at capturing local peaks and troughs).
  5. Record Smoothed Value: The algorithm evaluates the fitted polynomial at the exact target coordinate $x_0$ to obtain $\hat{y}_0$. The rest of this local regression line is immediately discarded!
  6. Slide and Repeat: The algorithm advances to the next $x$-value and repeats steps 1–5 from scratch. Connecting all recorded $\hat{y}$ points forms the continuous smoothed curve.
Historical Note — Savitzky–Golay Filter Equivalence: In analytical chemistry, spectroscopy, and digital signal processing, LOESS is widely known as the Savitzky–Golay filter, which was published by Abraham Savitzky and Marcel J. E. Golay in 1964 (15 years before Cleveland's 1979 LOESS paper). Both methods share the identical core mechanism: fitting localized low-degree polynomials via moving weighted least squares.

3. Key Tuning Parameters & Real-World Scatterplot Application

LOESS behavior is controlled by two primary parameters:

  • Span ($\alpha$ or bandwidth): The fraction of total data points included in each local regression. Small $\alpha$ ($0.1–0.2$) creates an erratic, highly wiggly curve that overfits local noise. Large $\alpha$ ($0.7–0.9$) creates an ultra-stiff curve that washes out real inflection points.
  • Polynomial Degree ($d$ or $\lambda$): Degree 1 (LOWESS line) vs. Degree 2 (LOESS parabola). Degree 2 is favored when datasets contain inflection points or curvature.
Case Study: Fuel-Tank Capacity vs. Car Price (Wilke Fig 14-4, Robin Lock 1993):

In a scatterplot of 93 automobiles released in 1993, fuel-tank capacity (gallons) is plotted against vehicle retail price ($).

  1. Non-Linear Discovery: A LOESS smooth immediately reveals an economic threshold: for budget cars (<$20,000), fuel tank capacity scales linearly with price.
  2. The Plateau: Above $20,000, the LOESS curve completely flattens out. Buying an expensive luxury car does not yield a larger fuel tank.
  3. Model-Free Power: The analyst did not need to guess a mathematical saturation function; LOESS revealed the threshold purely from localized data weighting.
Critical Limitations of LOESS:
  • Computational Cost: LOESS requires fitting a brand-new matrix regression for every point you wish to visualize ($O(n^2)$ complexity). It scales poorly to massive datasets ($>100,000$ points).
  • Zero Extrapolation: LOESS cannot predict values outside the observed minimum and maximum $x$-range. Because it possesses no global mathematical equation, attempting to extrapolate beyond the data boundaries is mathematically undefined.

Self-Assessment Practice Questions

Question 1 (Easy): What is the primary cause of artificial wiggles in moving averages that LOESS successfully eliminates?
Answer: The abrupt, equal weighting of points inside the sliding window box filter.

Explanation: Moving averages assign identical weights ($1/k$) to all points in the window and 0 to points outside. When an extreme outlier crosses the window boundary, the average abruptly jumps. LOESS eliminates this by using smooth tricube weighting where boundary points smoothly taper to weight zero.

Question 2 (Medium): What happens to the LOESS curve when the span parameter ($\alpha$) approaches 1.0 vs. when it approaches 0.05?
Answer: At $\alpha \to 1.0$, the curve becomes ultra-smooth and stiff (approaching a global linear/quadratic regression). At $\alpha \to 0.05$, the curve becomes extremely wiggly and overfits random noise.

Explanation: Span controls the neighborhood size. A small span includes few points, capturing high-frequency noise (high variance). A large span includes nearly all points, flattening local trends and introducing high bias.

Question 3 (Hard): Why does LOESS discard the local regression line immediately after calculating $\hat{y}_0$ for target $x_0$?
Answer: Because the local regression model is valid only at that localized neighborhood around $x_0$; each target $x$ requires its own uniquely weighted regression.

Explanation: LOESS is a non-parametric "lazy learner." It never builds a single global function. The polynomial fit at $x_0$ is only optimized for points around $x_0$. To evaluate $x_0 + \Delta x$, a new set of weights and a new regression must be computed from scratch.

Topic 3 Key Takeaways

  • Moving Average Deficiencies: Box filtering causes boundary wiggles, loses boundary points, and forces a bad noise-vs-lag trade-off.
  • LOESS 6-Step Engine: Pick target $x_0$ $\to$ define span window $\alpha$ $\to$ compute tricube weights $\to$ fit local WLS (degree 1 or 2) $\to$ record $\hat{y}_0$ $\to$ discard model and repeat.
  • Tricube Weighting: $W(u) = (1 - |u|^3)^3$ smoothly damps influence to zero at neighborhood edges.
  • Trade-Offs: Superb visual quality and model-free flexibility, but computationally expensive and unable to extrapolate.

4Splines vs. LOESS: Mechanics, Penalties & Efficiency

Piecewise cubic polynomials, knot continuity constraints, roughness penalty ($\lambda$), and Eager vs. Lazy learning (Wilke Ch. 14 & Handout)

While LOESS produces visually pleasing curves, its computational overhead becomes prohibitive as datasets expand. Spline smoothing solves this bottleneck through piecewise polynomial mathematics, providing instantaneous predictions once trained. Understanding why splines outpace LOESS reveals fundamental architectural concepts in statistical learning.

Smoothing Spline: A curve constructed by dividing the independent variable into segments and fitting low-degree polynomials (typically cubic) within each segment, joined smoothly at boundaries called knots.
Knots: The boundary locations along the $x$-axis that separate adjacent piecewise polynomial segments.
Generalized Additive Model (GAM): A flexible regression modeling framework that replaces standard linear predictors with sum of smooth spline functions: $y = \beta_0 + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + \dots + \epsilon$.
Intuitive one-liner: Splines are piecewise curves welded together so smoothly at joints (knots) that the seams are mathematically invisible.

1. The Three Continuity Constraints: Mathematical "Welding"

To prevent a piecewise curve from looking disjointed or jagged where segments meet at knot $t_k$, the algorithm enforces three strict mathematical continuity constraints:

1. Position ($C^0$ Continuity) No Gaps

The curves must touch at the boundary: $f_i(t_k) = f_{i+1}(t_k)$. Prevents vertical tears or steps.

2. Slope ($C^1$ Continuity) No Kinks

The first derivatives must match: $f'_i(t_k) = f'_{i+1}(t_k)$. Prevents sharp corners or angles.

3. Curvature ($C^2$ Continuity) Seamless Bend

The second derivatives must match: $f''_i(t_k) = f''_{i+1}(t_k)$. Guarantees smooth rate of bending across the seam.

2. The Roughness Penalty & Loss Function

In a true smoothing spline, a knot is placed at every unique $x$-value in the dataset. If left unconstrained, a cubic spline with $N$ knots would simply violently zig-zag to interpolate every single point (catastrophic overfitting). To prevent this, the algorithm minimizes a dual-objective loss function with a roughness penalty:

$\text{Loss} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - f(x_i)\right)^2}_{\text{Sum of Squared Errors (Fit)}} \;+\; \lambda \underbrace{\int \left[f''(x)\right]^2 dx}_{\text{Roughness Penalty (Curvature)}}$
Smoothing Parameter ($\lambda$) Behavior of Loss Function Resulting Spline Fit
$\lambda \to 0$ (Near Zero) Curvature penalty is ignored; algorithm only minimizes squared residuals. Overfit: Spline violently interpolates all points, creating a hyper-wiggly curve.
$\lambda \to \infty$ (Extremely Large) Curvature penalty dominates; algorithm forces $\int [f''(x)]^2 dx \to 0$, eliminating all bending. Linear Regression: With zero curvature ($f''(x) = 0$), the spline collapses into a single straight line!
Optimal $\lambda$ (Balanced) Balances fidelity to data against curve stiffness. Selected automatically by statistical packages using Generalized Cross-Validation (GCV).

3. Eager Learning vs. Lazy Learning: Why Splines Outperform LOESS

The difference between Splines and LOESS represents a classical dichotomy in computer science:

Feature / Dimension Spline Smoothing (Eager Learning) LOESS / LOWESS (Lazy Learning)
Learning Paradigm Eager: Solves global system of equations during training. Lazy: Merely memorizes data; delays all computation to query time.
Training Overhead Moderate upfront matrix inversion to compute polynomial $\beta$ coefficients. Virtually zero upfront computation.
Prediction Speed (Inference) Instantaneous ($O(1)$): Plugs query $x$ into segment equation: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3$. Extremely Slow ($O(n)$): Must calculate weights, invert local matrices, and fit a new WLS for every single predicted point.
Raw Data Retention Discards training points. Only stores small lookup table of knot coordinates and $\beta$ coefficients. Must retain entire raw dataset in memory indefinitely to calculate neighbor distances.
Scalability Handles millions of inference points effortlessly. Chokes on large datasets ($>50,000$ points).
The Spline Uncertainty Trap (Wilke Fig 14-5): Be cautious when presenting spline curves in publications. Different spline architectures (cubic regression splines, B-splines, thin-plate splines, Gaussian process splines) and different knot choices produce radically different boundary behaviors on identical datasets. Readers must be informed of the exact spline model and knot count used, because default software settings can drastically alter visual conclusions.

Self-Assessment Practice Questions

Question 1 (Easy): What mathematical curve does a smoothing spline collapse into when the smoothing parameter $\lambda$ approaches infinity?
Answer: A standard straight-line linear regression ($y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$).

Explanation: As $\lambda \to \infty$, the roughness penalty $\lambda \int [f''(x)]^2 dx$ dominates the loss function. To prevent the loss from exploding to infinity, the algorithm must force the curvature $f''(x) = 0$ everywhere. A function with zero second derivative is strictly a straight line.

Question 2 (Medium): Why can a fitted spline model make predictions without access to the original training data, while LOESS cannot?
Answer: Because splines are "eager learners" that encapsulate the entire trend into a compact global set of pre-calculated polynomial coefficients for each segment.

Explanation: Once the knot boundaries and polynomial $\beta$ values are determined, the original points are unnecessary; predicting $y$ only requires evaluating that segment's polynomial. LOESS is a "lazy learner" that has no saved coefficients; it must re-scan the raw data to compute distances and weights for every single query point.

Question 3 (Hard): What are the three continuity constraints enforced at spline knots, and what visual flaw would occur if $C^1$ continuity were omitted?
Answer: 1) $C^0$ (equal position / function value), 2) $C^1$ (equal slope / first derivative), and 3) $C^2$ (equal curvature / second derivative). Omitting $C^1$ continuity would result in sharp kinks or corners where polynomial segments meet.

Explanation: If slopes differ at knot $t_k$, the incoming segment arrives at one angle and the outgoing segment departs at another, creating an abrupt corner. Enforcing $f'_i(t_k) = f'_{i+1}(t_k)$ guarantees a perfectly smooth slope across the seam.

Topic 4 Key Takeaways

  • Piecewise Cubic Structure: Splines divide the domain into segments bounded by knots, enforcing $C^0$ (value), $C^1$ (slope), and $C^2$ (curvature) continuity.
  • Roughness Penalty: Loss balances Sum of Squared Errors against $\lambda \int [f''(x)]^2 dx$. $\lambda \to 0$ overfits; $\lambda \to \infty$ yields a linear regression line.
  • Eager vs. Lazy Learning: Splines compute coefficients upfront and discard raw data for instant $O(1)$ inference; LOESS recomputes WLS at every query point.
  • Software Defaults Warning: Spline types (cubic, thin-plate, Gaussian) vary widely at edges; always document knot counts and parameters.

5Functional Forms, The OLS Trap & Data Straightening

The Sum-of-Squares exponential trap, log-transform linearization, Log-Linear, Log-Log, Linear-Log plots, and base conversions (Wilke Ch. 14 & Handout)

General smoothers (like LOESS or splines) produce flexible curves, but their parameters lack physical or theoretical interpretability. In contrast, fitting an explicit analytical model (such as an exponential, power-law, or logarithmic equation) provides meaningful constants that describe governing natural laws. However, fitting non-linear curves directly to raw data using standard regression algorithms contains a notorious mathematical trap.

Data Straightening: The technique of applying mathematical transformations (most commonly logarithms) to the independent axis ($x$), the dependent axis ($y$), or both, such that non-linear curves are mapped into straight lines ($Y = mX + c$).
Power Law: A functional relationship where one quantity varies as a constant power of another ($y = c x^\alpha$), characterized by scale invariance.
Intuitive one-liner: Human eyes are terrible at evaluating curves, but brilliant at spotting straight lines. Straightening data lets our eyes detect minute anomalies.

1. The "Sum of Squares" Trap in Direct Non-Linear Fitting

When statistical software fits a non-linear curve directly to raw data points, it almost universally uses Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), seeking parameters that minimize the total sum of squared residuals: $\text{SSE} = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$.

The OLS Distortion Mechanism (Wilke Fig 14-8 & 14-9):

In an exponentially growing time series (such as preprint submissions or epidemic cases), late-stage observations are orders of magnitude larger than early-stage observations:

  • Late Points Dominate: An error of $1,000$ on a late point produces a catastrophic squared penalty of $1,000^2 = 1,000,000$.
  • Early Points Are Sacrificed: An error of $5$ on an early point produces a negligible squared penalty of $5^2 = 25$.
  • Systematic Bias: To minimize the total sum, the algorithm aggressively bends the curve to fit the massive late-stage numbers, happily sacrificing the early points. Consequently, the fitted curve systematically overshoots or undershoots early observations.
The Log-Transform Solution — Equalizing Proportional Error: Instead of fitting a non-linear curve directly to raw $y$, take the logarithm of the values first ($\ln y$) and fit a standard linear regression: $\ln(y) = \ln(a) + bx$.
Because $\ln(y_1) - \ln(y_2) = \ln(y_1 / y_2)$, logarithmic differences represent relative percentage changes rather than absolute additive differences. A $10\%$ deviation on a small value receives the exact same mathematical penalty as a $10\%$ deviation on a massive value. Proportional fidelity is preserved across the entire time series!

Wilke's Golden Axiom: "It is usually better to fit a straight line to transformed data than to fit a nonlinear curve to untransformed data."

3. Real-World Case Studies with Numerical Walk-Throughs

Case Study 1: Log-Linear Straightening — Bacterial Doubling

A bacterial culture doubles every hour ($y = 2^x$).

  • Original Raw Data (Curved): $x = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]$,  $y = [2, 4, 8, 16, 32]$.
  • Transformation: Leave $x$ linear; compute natural log of $y$: $\ln(y) = [0.693, 1.386, 2.079, 2.773, 3.466]$.
  • Resulting Linear Model: $Y = 0.693 x + 0$. The slope is exactly $\ln(2) \approx 0.6931$, representing the hourly continuous compound growth rate.
Case Study 2: Log-Log Straightening — Kleiber’s Law of Metabolic Scaling

In biology, Max Kleiber demonstrated that an animal's basal metabolic rate ($y$, kcal/day) scales as the $3/4$ power of its body mass ($x$, kg): $y = 50 x^{0.75}$.

  • Original Raw Data (Curved): Mass $x = [1, 10, 100, 1000]$,  Metabolism $y = [50, 281, 1581, 8891]$.
  • Transformation: Take the log of both axes: $\ln(x) = [0, 2.30, 4.61, 6.91]$,  $\ln(y) = [3.91, 5.64, 7.37, 9.09]$.
  • Resulting Linear Model: $Y = 0.75 X + 3.91$. The slope ($0.75$) exactly unmasks the theoretical sub-linear scaling exponent.
Case Study 3: Linear-Log Straightening — Diminishing Returns in Marketing

Spending $\$100$ attracts $50$ customers; spending $\$1,000$ attracts $100$; spending $\$10,000$ attracts $150$: $y = 50 \log_{10}(x)$.

  • Transformation: Compress the $x$-axis using $\log_{10}(x)$: $\log_{10}(x) = [1, 2, 3, 4]$,  $y = [50, 100, 150, 200]$.
  • Resulting Linear Model: $Y = 50 X + 0$. The slope ($50$) tells the VP of Marketing: "Every time you multiply advertising budget by 10×, you obtain exactly 50 additional customers."

4. The "Programming Trap" & Base Conversions

The Programming Trap: In high school mathematics, $\log(x)$ implies base 10 ($\log_{10}$). However, in virtually all programming languages (Python `math.log` / `numpy.log`, R `log()`, C++, Java), `log(x)` defaults to the natural logarithm (base $e \approx 2.71828$)!
To obtain base 10 in Python or R, you must explicitly call log10(x). Calling `log(x)` when expecting base 10 introduces a hidden error factor of $\ln(10) \approx 2.3026$!
Logarithm Base Standard Notation Primary Use Cases
Base 10 $\log_{10}(x)$ Human orders of magnitude: Richter earthquake scale, chemical pH values, acoustic decibels, financial orders of magnitude.
Natural Base $e$ $\ln(x)$ or $\log_e(x)$ Calculus and rates: Continuous compound growth, derivatives, statistical regression modeling, physics decay laws.
Base 2 $\log_2(x)$ Computer science: Binary search algorithms ($O(\log_2 n)$), tree depths, information theory (bits/entropy), doubling times.
Mathematical Base Conversion Formulas:
  • Change-of-Base Formula: To calculate base-10 using natural logs on a calculator: $$\log_{10}(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(10)} \approx \frac{\ln(x)}{2.3026}$$
  • Converting Any Exponential Base $a$ to Natural Base $e$: Any exponential $y = a^x$ can be rewritten as continuous growth $y = e^{kx}$ by setting $k = \ln(a)$.
    Example: Convert $y = 10^x$ to base $e$: since $\ln(10) \approx 2.3026$, $y = 10^x \iff y = e^{2.3026 x}$. Both equations produce the identical curve!

Self-Assessment Practice Questions

Question 1 (Easy): Which axis or axes must be transformed logarithmically to straighten a power-law relationship ($y = c x^\alpha$)?
Answer: Both the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis (a Log-Log plot).

Explanation: Taking the logarithm of both sides of $y = c x^\alpha$ yields $\ln(y) = \ln(c) + \alpha \ln(x)$. Plotting $\ln(y)$ against $\ln(x)$ results in a straight line with slope $\alpha$ and intercept $\ln(c)$.

Question 2 (Medium): Why does fitting an exponential curve directly to raw data with OLS systematically misfit early observations?
Answer: Because OLS minimizes squared absolute residuals ($\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$). Large values at the tail produce massive squared penalties, forcing the optimizer to fit large numbers while ignoring small errors on early points.

Explanation: In raw units, an error of 100 on an early small value (say 10) is a 1000% error, but in absolute squared terms ($10,000$) it is dwarfed by a 2% error on a point of 100,000 (absolute error $2,000 \implies$ squared penalty $4,000,000$). Log-transforming first converts differences to proportional ratios, equalizing weights.

Question 3 (Hard): In Python, a student executes `slope = np.polyfit(x, np.log(y), 1)[0]` on bacterial doubling data ($y = 2^x$). What numerical slope will Python return, and why does it differ from 1.0?
Answer: Python will return approximately $0.6931$ (which is $\ln(2)$), rather than $1.0$.

Explanation: `np.log` computes the natural logarithm (base $e$), not base 2 or base 10. The equation is $\ln(y) = \ln(2^x) = x \cdot \ln(2) \approx 0.6931 x$. If the student wanted a slope of 1.0, they would need to use `np.log2(y)`.

Topic 5 Key Takeaways

  • The OLS Exponential Trap: Fitting curves directly to raw exponential data produces heavy bias; log-transforming first ensures equal proportional weighting across all scales.
  • The 3 Straightening Transforms: Log-Linear straightens exponentials (slope = growth rate $b$); Log-Log straightens power laws (slope = exponent $\alpha$); Linear-Log straightens logarithms (slope = gain per 10×).
  • The Programming Trap: In Python/R, `log()` is base $e$ ($\ln$). Explicitly use `log10()` for orders of magnitude.
  • Base Conversions: $\log_{10}(x) = \ln(x) / \ln(10)$; and $a^x = e^{kx}$ where $k = \ln(a)$.

6Detrending & Time-Series Decomposition (Keeling Curve)

Multiplicative detrending, the 3 time-series components, STL by LOESS, and the Mauna Loa CO₂ Keeling curve (Wilke Ch. 14)

When a time series exhibits a powerful secular upward or downward trajectory, the sheer magnitude of the long-term trend can swallow vital cyclical behaviors, seasonal fluctuations, and unexpected economic or environmental shocks. Detrending and time-series decomposition isolate these distinct temporal forces.

Detrending: The mathematical removal of the dominant long-term baseline trend from a time series to highlight unexpected deviations, bubbles, or cyclical oscillations.
Time-Series Decomposition: The process of deconstructing an observed series into three distinct additive or multiplicative structural components: Long-Term Trend, Seasonal/Cyclical Variations, and the Remainder.
STL Decomposition: Seasonal and Trend decomposition using LOESS (Cleveland et al. 1990), a versatile non-parametric algorithm that uses iterative LOESS smoothers to separate trend from seasonality.
Intuitive one-liner: Decomposition dissects a messy signal into its slow climate (trend), its annual seasons (cycle), and its unpredictable daily weather (noise).

1. Detrending: Unmasking Hidden Bubbles (Freddie Mac Case Study)

In economics, assets often compound in percentage increments (exponential growth). The Freddie Mac House Price Index tracks relative housing values across US states (indexed to $100$ in Dec 2000).

The California Housing Illusion (Wilke Fig 14-10 & 14-11):
  • The Raw Time Series: Between 1980 and 2017, California home prices grew along an exponential trend of $4.7\%$ annually (represented by a straight gray line on a logarithmic $y$-axis). Between 1990 and 1998, California prices appeared to decline only modestly in raw dollar terms.
  • The Multiplicative Detrending Formula: $$\text{Detrended Index}_t = \frac{\text{Actual Index}_t}{\text{Expected Trend}_t}$$ On a logarithmic scale, dividing raw values is graphically identical to subtracting the straight trend line.
  • The Revelation: Relative to what buyers expected based on continuous 4.7% compounding, California home prices actually collapsed by 25% at their lowest point in 1998! Detrending unmasked a massive housing bubble and severe bust that was completely camouflaged in the raw chart.
  • Cross-State Comparison: Nevada showed a single extreme speculative bubble in the mid-2000s, whereas Texas and West Virginia tracked their $2.8\%$ annual trends with virtually zero speculative deviation.

2. The Three Structural Components of a Time Series

Classical decomposition expresses any observed time series $Y_t$ as the mathematical combination of three distinct signals:

Component Mathematical Role Underlying Real-World Mechanism Timescale
1. Long-Term Trend ($T_t$) The smooth, secular direction of the data over decades or centuries. Population growth, economic expansion, technological evolution, global greenhouse gas accumulation. Decades / Centuries
2. Seasonal Variation ($S_t$) Repetitive, periodic oscillations with known constant period. Calendar events, summer vs. winter temperatures, annual agricultural harvests, holiday retail surges. Daily, Weekly, or Annual (Months/Quarters)
3. Remainder / Noise ($R_t$) The residual difference: $R_t = Y_t - (T_t + S_t)$. Stochastic white noise, random measuring errors, or unique catastrophic external shocks (e.g., massive volcanic eruptions, pandemic shutdowns, wars). Unpredictable / High Frequency

3. Case Study: The Mauna Loa Keeling Curve

Monitored continuously since 1958 by Charles David Keeling at 11,135 feet elevation atop the Mauna Loa volcano in Hawaii, the Keeling Curve provides the definitive empirical proof of global climate change.

Biological Mechanism of the Seasonal Oscillation: Why does atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ dip every single northern summer?
  • Hemispheric Asymmetry: The Earth's landmasses and terrestrial forests are heavily concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • Photosynthetic Drawdown: From May through September, northern deciduous forests burst into leaf. Intense photosynthesis consumes billions of tons of atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$, causing global concentrations to drop by $\sim 8\text{ ppm}$.
  • Winter Respiration & Decay: From October through April, northern plant foliage dies and soil respiration releases stored carbon back into the atmosphere, causing global concentrations to climb back to their annual peak.

Self-Assessment Practice Questions

Question 1 (Easy): In the STL decomposition of a time series, what does the "Remainder" component capture?
Answer: Random high-frequency noise, measurement errors, and sudden unique external shock events (e.g., volcanic eruptions, sudden financial crises).

Explanation: The remainder is what remains after subtracting the long-term secular trend and regular seasonal cycles ($R_t = Y_t - T_t - S_t$). If no catastrophic shock occurs, it resembles white noise.

Question 2 (Medium): Why did detrending California's housing price index reveal a 25% collapse in 1998, even though raw dollar prices declined only modestly?
Answer: Because California was on an expected exponential growth trajectory of 4.7% annually; relative to where prices should have been, actual prices were 25% lower.

Explanation: Raw prices reflect inflation and baseline compounding. When prices merely stay flat or drop slightly during an era of expected 4.7% compound growth, the shortfall compounds over 8 years, representing a massive destruction of real asset value.

Question 3 (Hard): How does the STL algorithm separate the seasonal component from the trend component without requiring a predefined mathematical equation?
Answer: By applying an alternating, iterative sequence of LOESS smoothers across seasonal sub-series and across the overall timeline.

Explanation: STL uses LOESS smoothing within each monthly sub-series (e.g., all Januaries across years) to extract the seasonal pattern, then applies a long-window LOESS smoother to the seasonally adjusted data to isolate the secular trend.

Topic 6 Key Takeaways

  • Detrending: Dividing raw values by trend removes dominant growth, unmasking hidden cyclical crashes (e.g., California's 25% relative housing drop in 1998).
  • 3 Core Signals: Any time series is decomposed into Long-Term Trend ($T_t$), Seasonal Cycles ($S_t$), and Remainder Noise ($R_t$).
  • STL by LOESS: Iterative non-parametric filtering that extracts flexible trends and seasonal shapes without rigid mathematical equations.
  • Keeling Curve Architecture: Demonstrates a >50 ppm secular rise, an 8 ppm annual biosphere breathing cycle, and <1.6 ppm random noise.

4. Top 10 Red-Flag Exam Traps & Anti-Patterns

  1. The Non-Zero Baseline in Area Charts: An area chart whose vertical axis does not start at absolute zero ($y=0$) is statistically invalid because the shaded pixel area misrepresents relative physical magnitudes.
  2. The OLS Sum-of-Squares Exponential Trap: Fitting an exponential curve directly to raw data with OLS produces heavy bias because huge squared errors on large late points dominate the loss function, sacrificing early small points. Always fit a straight line to log-transformed data instead.
  3. The Meaningless Scribble in Connected Scatterplots: Omitting directional arrowheads/color gradients and key date landmarks turns a connected scatterplot into an illegible tangle of spaghetti.
  4. The Python `log()` Trap: In Python (`math.log`, `np.log`) and R, `log()` computes the natural logarithm ($\ln$), NOT base 10! Base 10 requires `log10()`.
  5. LOESS Cannot Extrapolate: Because LOESS builds local models point-by-point without a global analytical function, it is mathematically incapable of making predictions outside the domain of observed $x$-values.
  6. Moving Average Boundary Wiggles: Moving average wiggles are NOT real data trends; they are mathematical artifacts caused by abrupt $0 \leftrightarrow 1/k$ transitions at window edges.
  7. Connecting Unordered Categories: Connecting points with a line is only legitimate if the independent axis possesses a strict, continuous physical order (time, dose). Never connect nominal categorical categories!
  8. Splines at $\lambda \to \infty$: As the roughness penalty $\lambda \to \infty$, the spline does NOT disappear; it collapses into a standard straight-line linear regression because its second derivative is forced to zero everywhere.
  9. Reading Connected Scatterplot Vectors: Up-Right ($\nearrow$) = Positive Correlation; Up-Left to Down-Right ($\searrow$) = Anticorrelation; Loops/Spirals = Cyclical phase-lagging cycles.
  10. The Biological Cause of the Keeling Cycle: The annual ~8 ppm dip in global atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ occurs during Northern Hemisphere summer because Earth's landmasses and terrestrial forests are overwhelmingly concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere, where massive seasonal photosynthesis draws down carbon.
