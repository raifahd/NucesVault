Visualizing Time Series & Trends

Textbook & Lecture References Claus O. Wilke: Fundamentals of Data Visualization, Chapter 13 (Time Series & Independent Variables) & Chapter 14 (Visualizing Trends) • Course Handout: Notes on Time Series and Trends (Local Regression, LOESS, Splines, Eager vs. Lazy Computation, Data Straightening & Conversions).

1Time Series Foundations & Line Graph Principles

Temporal ordering, points vs. lines, shaded area baselines, direct labeling, and dose–response curves (Wilke Ch. 13)

Time Series: Data recorded over ordered intervals of an independent variable (t). Unlike general scatterplots where observation order is arbitrary, every observation in a time series (except the endpoints) possesses a strictly defined predecessor and successor.
Line Graph: Connects consecutive points with straight segments to guide the visual system along the trajectory of change.

1. The 4 Visual Modes of a Time Series

When visualizing time series, how you render the data depends directly on sample density and whether observations are discrete events or continuous processes:

  • Dots Only (Scatter): Displays raw, isolated measurements. Best used when sampling is sparse, irregularly spaced, or when you explicitly want the audience to evaluate each observation independently without implying continuity between points.
  • Dots + Connecting Lines: The connecting lines serve as perceptual guides rather than measured data. They explicitly emphasize chronological order and make slope changes easy to track, while the dots preserve the exact locations of real measurements. Ideal for small to moderate datasets (e.g. monthly revenue).
  • Lines Only (No Dots): The gold standard for dense time series (e.g. daily stock tickers, hourly sensor logs). When hundreds of observations are plotted, individual dots produce visual clutter and ink bloat. Eliminating dots lets the human eye focus strictly on macro-trends, cyclical oscillations, and curvature.
  • Filled Area Under the Curve: Emphasizes cumulative volume, total mass, or stock volume rather than just instantaneous rates.

The 4 Visual Modes & The Zero-Baseline Law

Exam Rule — The Zero-Baseline Law: An area chart whose vertical axis does not start at absolute zero (y = 0) is statistically deceptive. Shaded pixel area represents total magnitude. If the axis starts at 50, a value of 60 appears twice as large as a value of 55, creating a gross visual distortion of proportion.

2. Direct Labeling vs. Legends & Dose–Response Curves

  • Direct Labeling > External Legends: When plotting multiple time series (2 to 5 curves), external legends force saccadic eye movements — the reader must continuously jump back and forth between the line and the legend box to match colors or symbols. Placing labels directly next to the terminal end of each curve eliminates cognitive lookup fatigue and removes the need for artificial dot shapes (Wilke Fig 13-7).
  • Dose–Response Curves: Line graphs are not restricted to calendar dates. They apply to any ordered, continuous physical treatment variable. A classic benchmark is the Yates (1935) agricultural experiment, which plotted oat yield against nitrogen fertilizer dose (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 cwt/acre). The vertical intercept represents baseline unfertilized yield, while the slope reveals the marginal crop response rate.
  • Never Connect Unordered Categories: Connecting points with a line is only legitimate if the independent axis has an inherent physical or continuous sequence. Connecting unordered nominal categories (e.g. eye color, product brands) implies a continuous trajectory that does not exist.

Practice Questions (Instant Self-Check)

Q1: Under what exact condition is shading the area under a curve statistically valid?

Answer: Only when the vertical (y) axis begins at absolute zero (y = 0), ensuring shaded pixel area scales proportionally with physical quantities.

Q2: Why does Wilke strongly prefer direct labeling over external legends for 2–5 line series?

Answer: Direct labeling places labels right where the lines end, eliminating cognitive load from saccadic eye movements and removing the need to memorize distinct dot symbols or color swatches.

Q3: When should data dots be omitted entirely in a time series chart?

Answer: When data is densely sampled (hundreds of observations), where dots create visual ink clutter and distract from the overarching macro-trend and curvature.

Topic 1 Key Takeaways

  • Lines are Guides: Connecting segments guide the eye along sequence; omit dots on dense continuous data.
  • Zero-Baseline Law: Area graphs MUST anchor at y = 0. Truncating the vertical baseline misrepresents physical volume.
  • Direct Labels: Place labels directly at the end of lines to eliminate cognitive lookup load.

2Multiple Response Variables & Connected Scatterplots

Dual time series, phase portraits, cyclical spirals, readability trade-offs, and PCA trajectories (Wilke Ch. 13)

Connected Scatterplot (Phase Portrait): Maps two response variables on Cartesian axes (x and y) and connects consecutive observations in chronological order with directed line segments.
Phase Space: A coordinate space where system states are plotted as coordinates, and time is traced as a continuous trajectory path.

1. Stacked Synchronized Lines vs. Connected Scatterplots

When monitoring two interrelated time series (e.g. unemployment rate and house price changes), data scientists face a choice of representation:

  • Dual-Axis Graphs (Anti-Pattern): Putting two different scales on left and right vertical axes is highly discouraged because changing axis scaling artificially shifts the visual intersection point of the two curves.
  • Stacked Synchronized Line Plots: Stacking two separate plots with a shared horizontal time axis makes it easy to read individual values at any given date, but detecting whether one variable leads or lags the other requires tedious mental subtraction of slopes across charts.
  • Connected Scatterplots (Phase Portraits): By removing time from the axes and treating it as a path, cyclical boom-and-bust dynamics and feedback loops immediately emerge as geometric spirals.

Stacked Synchronized Lines vs. Connected Scatterplot Phase Dynamics

Practice Questions (Instant Self-Check)

Q1: What does an upper-left to lower-right (↘) vector signify on a connected scatterplot?

Answer: An anticorrelated trade-off state (Δy / Δx < 0), where variable x increases while variable y decreases.

Q2: What two elements are mandatory to prevent a connected scatterplot from becoming a scribble?

Answer: 1) Clear directional cues (arrowheads or color gradients), and 2) Chronological landmark date labels along the path.

Q3: What key finding did Haroz et al. (2016) discover about connected scatterplots?

Answer: While readers make more interpretation errors regarding chronology than with line graphs, connected scatterplots generate significantly higher reader engagement and clearly communicate cyclical feedback loops.

Topic 2 Key Takeaways

  • Trajectories in Phase Space: Connected scatterplots plot two metrics against each other; time becomes a directed path.
  • Directional Vectors: ↗ = positive correlation; ↘ = anticorrelation / trade-off; loops = cyclical phase lags.
  • Design Law: Always include arrowheads/gradients and landmark date labels.

3Smoothing Techniques & Local Regression (LOESS)

Moving average limitations, the 6-step LOESS algorithm, tricube weighting, span tuning, and Savitzky–Golay connection (Wilke Ch. 14 & Handout)

Moving Average: Replaces each observation with the arithmetic mean of k surrounding points inside a sliding window.
LOESS / LOWESS: Locally Estimated / Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing (Cleveland 1979). A non-parametric regression method that fits localized, weighted polynomials point-by-point.

1. Why Moving Averages Fail (Box Filter Flaws)

The simplest smoothing technique is a moving average, but it suffers from severe mathematical and visual deficiencies:

  • Boundary Wiggles: Because a moving average uses equal weighting (1/k box filter), when an extreme outlier enters or exits the boundary of the sliding window, the smoothed line takes an abrupt artificial jerk. These wiggles reflect window edge artifacts rather than real phenomena.
  • Edge Data Loss (Clipping): A centered moving average of window size k cannot compute values for the first k/2 or last k/2 points in the series, clipping critical recent data.
  • Noise vs. Lag Dilemma: A narrow window (e.g. 10 days) preserves high-frequency noise; a wide window (e.g. 100 days) severely lags behind actual turning points and peaks.

Moving Average (Box Filter) vs. LOESS Tricube Bell Curve

2. The 6-Step LOESS Algorithm

LOESS solves the box filter problem by applying continuous distance weighting and local regression:

  1. Select Target Location (x0): The point on the x-axis where the smoothed estimate ŷ0 is desired.
  2. Define Neighborhood Window (Span α): Select the nearest k = αn observations around x0. The span α (typically between 0.2 and 0.8) controls the bias-variance trade-off: small α yields high flexibility (low bias, high variance); large α yields stiff smoothing (high bias, low variance).
  3. Compute Tricube Distance Weights: Let Δ(x0) be the distance to the farthest neighbor in the window. Compute normalized distance u = |xi − x0| / Δ(x0). Assign weights via the Tricube Weight Function:
    W(u) = (1 - |u|³)³  for |u| ≤ 1,  and W(u) = 0 for |u| > 1
    Points closest to x0 receive maximum weight ≈ 1, while weights taper smoothly to exactly 0 at the window edge, eliminating boundary wiggles!
  4. Fit Local Weighted Regression (WLS): Fit a low-degree polynomial minimizing ∑ wi (yi − ŷi)2.
    • Degree 1 (LOWESS): Fits a local straight line (y = mx + c).
    • Degree 2 (LOESS): Fits a local quadratic parabola (y = ax² + bx + c), which is vastly superior for capturing true peaks and valleys.
  5. Record ŷ0 & Discard Local Model: Read the fitted value at x0. Discard the rest of the local regression line.
  6. Slide and Repeat: Advance to the next query coordinate and repeat steps 1–5 across the entire dataset.
Critical LOESS Facts:
1) Zero Extrapolation: Because LOESS builds no global mathematical formula, it is strictly incapable of predicting outside the observed range [min x, max x].
2) Savitzky–Golay Equivalence (1964): On evenly spaced data, LOESS is mathematically identical to the Savitzky–Golay digital filter used in signal processing and spectroscopy.
3) Computational Complexity: LOESS requires solving an independent matrix inversion for every query point, resulting in O(n2) computational cost.

Practice Questions (Instant Self-Check)

Q1: What mathematical property of the Tricube function eliminates moving average boundary wiggles?

Answer: The Tricube weight function W(u) = (1 − |u|³)³ smoothly tapers weights to zero at the boundary of the window, ensuring that data points entering or exiting the neighborhood transition continuously with zero abrupt derivative shock.

Q2: What is the core algorithmic difference between LOWESS and LOESS?

Answer: LOWESS fits a local degree-1 polynomial (straight line); LOESS fits a local degree-2 polynomial (quadratic parabola), enabling LOESS to faithfully capture peaks and valleys without flattening them.

Q3: Why can't LOESS extrapolate beyond the range of observed data?

Answer: LOESS is non-parametric; it does not estimate a global equation. Each prediction is computed purely from local neighbors, which do not exist outside the observed data range.

Topic 3 Key Takeaways

  • Box Filter Defect: Equal weighting causes artificial wiggles and clips k/2 edge points.
  • LOESS Engine: Target x0 → Span α → Tricube weights → Local WLS → Record ŷ0 → Discard model.
  • Zero Extrapolation: High visual quality, but computationally heavy and strictly confined to observed domain.

4Splines vs. LOESS: Mechanics, Penalties & Efficiency

Piecewise cubic polynomials, knot continuity constraints, roughness penalty (λ), and Eager vs. Lazy learning (Wilke Ch. 14 & Handout)

Smoothing Spline: Piecewise low-degree polynomials (typically cubic 3rd-degree) joined seamlessly at boundary coordinates called knots (k segments ⇒ k+1 knots).
Generalized Additive Model (GAM): Framework expressing a target response as a sum of smooth spline functions: y = β0 + ∑ fj(xj) + ε.

1. Knot Continuity Constraints & The Roughness Penalty

The term "spline" originates from physical naval drafting, where draftsmen bent thin, flexible strips of wood held in place by heavy lead weights ("ducks") to draw smooth ship hulls. In data visualization, mathematical splines stitch piecewise cubic polynomials together under three strict boundary constraints at every knot tk:

  • C0 Continuity (Value / Position): fi(tk) = fi+1(tk) ⇒ Adjacent polynomial segments must touch at the knot; no vertical gaps or step discontinuities.
  • C1 Continuity (Slope / First Derivative): f′i(tk) = f′i+1(tk) ⇒ Tangent slopes must match exactly; no sharp angles, cusps, or kinks.
  • C2 Continuity (Curvature / Second Derivative): f″i(tk) = f″i+1(tk) ⇒ Bending acceleration must match; transitions are mathematically seamless and visually invisible.
Loss = ∑i=1..n (yi − f(xi))2 + λ ∫ [f″(x)]2 dx
λ ParameterMathematical BehaviorResulting Fit
λ → 0Roughness penalty is ignored; optimizes residuals only.Overfit: Violently interpolates every point (hyper-wiggly).
λ → ∞Curvature forced to zero (∫ [f″(x)]2 dx → 0).Linear Regression: Collapses into a single straight line!
Optimal λBalances training fit against curve stiffness.Selected automatically via Generalized Cross-Validation (GCV).

Spline Knot Continuity & Eager vs. Lazy Learning Pipeline

2. Eager vs. Lazy Learning Architecture

The profound conceptual contrast tested on exams is computational architecture:

  • Splines (Eager Learner): Splines perform all mathematical work upfront by setting up and solving a banded, tridiagonal linear system. Once polynomial coefficients [β0, β1, β2, β3] are solved for each knot interval, the original training data is completely discarded. Evaluating any new prediction is instantaneous O(1) plug-and-chug.
  • LOESS (Lazy Learner): LOESS does zero upfront compilation. It merely memorizes the raw data in RAM. When a query is requested, it must locate neighbors, calculate tricube weights, and invert a local regression matrix on the fly. Predicting across millions of query points is prohibitively slow.

Practice Questions (Instant Self-Check)

Q1: What function does a smoothing spline collapse into as λ → ∞?

Answer: A standard straight-line linear regression (y = mx + c), because the roughness penalty forces second derivative curvature f″(x) = 0 everywhere across the domain.

Q2: What visual defect occurs if C1 continuity is omitted at a spline knot?

Answer: Sharp kinks, cusps, or abrupt corners where adjacent polynomial segments meet, due to unequal first derivatives (tangent slopes).

Q3: Why can splines make predictions without storing the original training dataset?

Answer: Splines are "eager learners" that solve the equations upfront and store compact polynomial coefficients (β), allowing raw training data to be safely discarded.

Topic 4 Key Takeaways

  • 3 Continuity Rules: Knots require C0 equal value, C1 equal slope, C2 equal curvature.
  • Roughness Penalty: λ → 0 overfits; λ → ∞ collapses into linear regression.
  • Eager vs. Lazy: Splines compute coefficients upfront (O(1) lookup); LOESS recomputes WLS at every query point.

5Functional Forms, The OLS Trap & Data Straightening

The Sum-of-Squares exponential trap, log-transform linearization, Log-Linear, Log-Log, Linear-Log plots, and base conversions (Wilke Ch. 14 & Handout)

Data Straightening: Applying coordinate transformations (typically logarithms) so that non-linear functional relationships map into straight lines (Y = mX + c).
Power Law: y = c·xα (variable is the base; exponent is constant) vs. Exponential: y = a·ebx (constant base; variable is the exponent).

1. The "Sum of Squares" Trap in Direct Non-Linear Fitting

When fitting non-linear data (such as COVID infection cases or compound interest), beginners often attempt to fit an exponential function directly to raw data using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). This leads to a severe mathematical trap:

  • The Trap: Standard OLS minimizes the sum of squared absolute errors: SSE = ∑ (yi − ŷi)2.
  • Tail Domination: In exponential curves, values span multiple orders of magnitude. An absolute error of 1,000 on a late large observation adds 1,000,000 to the loss function, while an absolute error of 5 on an early observation adds only 25.
  • Systematic Distortion: OLS is forced to bend aggressively to minimize the massive squared residuals of the tail points, completely sacrificing the small early points. As a result, the fitted curve systematically overshoots or undershoots initial data points (Wilke Fig 14-8 & 14-9).
  • The Log-Transform Solution: Taking the logarithm of y first (ln y = ln a + bx) and fitting a straight line minimizes relative percentage errors: [ln(y) − ln(ŷ)] = ln(y/ŷ). A 10% error at y = 10 produces the exact same loss penalty as a 10% error at y = 10,000!
Wilke's Golden Axiom: "It is usually better to fit a straight line to transformed data than to fit a nonlinear curve to untransformed data."

The OLS Distortion Trap & The 3 Data Straightening Transformations

Practice Questions (Instant Self-Check)

Q1: Which transformation straightens a power law (y = c·xα)?

Answer: Log-Log plot (ln x, ln y), where the slope directly equals power exponent α.

Q2: Why does direct non-linear OLS distort early exponential observations?

Answer: OLS minimizes absolute squared residuals; huge late points dominate the loss sum, forcing the curve to sacrifice early points to minimize tail error.

Q3: What slope does Python `np.polyfit(x, np.log(y), 1)` return on doubling data (y = 2x)?

Answer: 0.6931 (ln 2), because `np.log` computes base e, not base 2.

Topic 5 Key Takeaways

  • The OLS Trap: Raw exponential OLS is biased by large tail values; log-transform first.
  • 3 Transforms: Log-Linear (growth rate b); Log-Log (power exponent α); Linear-Log (gain per 10×).
  • Programming Trap: Python log() is base e (ln); use log10() for base 10.

6Detrending & Time-Series Decomposition (Keeling Curve)

Multiplicative detrending, the 3 time-series components, STL by LOESS, and the Mauna Loa CO₂ Keeling curve (Wilke Ch. 14)

Detrending: Removing the secular baseline trend (Actualt / Trendt) to isolate hidden cyclical crashes, bubbles, or shocks.
STL Decomposition: Seasonal and Trend decomposition using LOESS (Cleveland et al. 1990): Yt = Trendt + Seasonalt + Remaindert.

1. Detrending: Unmasking Hidden Crashes (California Housing)

Secular long-term growth frequently masks severe local crises. Claus O. Wilke illustrates this with Freddie Mac housing price indices (1980–2017):

  • The Illusion: Between 1990 and 1998, raw nominal California housing prices appeared flat and unremarkable.
  • The Detrending Insight: California's long-term compounding growth trend was 4.7% annually. When prices were multiplicatively detrended (Actualt / Trendt), it revealed a devastating 25% price collapse relative to trend!
  • Method: In multiplicative detrending, dividing by trend is equivalent to subtracting the linear trend line on a logarithmic scale.

STL Decomposition of the Mauna Loa CO₂ Keeling Curve

2. The 3 Decomposition Components (Keeling Curve Case Study)

ComponentTimescaleKeeling Curve Case Study Metric
Trend (Tt)Decades / Centuries> 50 ppm continuous increase (from ~350 to >415 ppm) due to fossil fuels.
Seasonal (St)Annual (Months)~8 ppm wave (±4 ppm): Northern summer photosynthesis draws CO₂ down; winter rot releases it.
Remainder (Rt)High Frequency< 1.6 ppm random noise; catches massive unique shocks (e.g. volcanic eruptions).

Practice Questions (Instant Self-Check)

Q1: Why did California housing represent a 25% drop when detrended?

Answer: Because the baseline compounded at 4.7% annually; flat nominal prices represented a 25% loss relative to the expected secular trend.

Q2: What causes the annual 8 ppm Keeling cycle dip every Northern summer?

Answer: Earth's forests and landmasses are concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere; summer photosynthesis actively draws down billions of tons of CO₂.

Q3: What would a sudden spike in the Remainder component signify?

Answer: A catastrophic external shock event (e.g., a massive volcanic eruption or pandemic shutdown).

Topic 6 Key Takeaways

  • Detrending: Dividing actual by trend exposes relative asset crashes hidden by inflation.
  • 3 Components: Yt = Trendt + Seasonalt + Remaindert.
  • Keeling Curve: >50 ppm trend rise, 8 ppm annual biosphere cycle, <1.6 ppm noise.

4. Top 10 Red-Flag Exam Traps & Anti-Patterns

  1. The Non-Zero Baseline in Area Charts: An area chart whose vertical axis does not start at absolute zero ($y=0$) is statistically invalid because the shaded pixel area misrepresents relative physical magnitudes.
  2. The OLS Sum-of-Squares Exponential Trap: Fitting an exponential curve directly to raw data with OLS produces heavy bias because huge squared errors on large late points dominate the loss function, sacrificing early small points. Always fit a straight line to log-transformed data instead.
  3. The Meaningless Scribble in Connected Scatterplots: Omitting directional arrowheads/color gradients and key date landmarks turns a connected scatterplot into an illegible tangle of spaghetti.
  4. The Python `log()` Trap: In Python (`math.log`, `np.log`) and R, `log()` computes the natural logarithm ($\ln$), NOT base 10! Base 10 requires `log10()`.
  5. LOESS Cannot Extrapolate: Because LOESS builds local models point-by-point without a global analytical function, it is mathematically incapable of making predictions outside the domain of observed $x$-values.
  6. Moving Average Boundary Wiggles: Moving average wiggles are NOT real data trends; they are mathematical artifacts caused by abrupt $0 \leftrightarrow 1/k$ transitions at window edges.
  7. Connecting Unordered Categories: Connecting points with a line is only legitimate if the independent axis possesses a strict, continuous physical order (time, dose). Never connect nominal categorical categories!
  8. Splines at $\lambda \to \infty$: As the roughness penalty $\lambda \to \infty$, the spline does NOT disappear; it collapses into a standard straight-line linear regression because its second derivative is forced to zero everywhere.
  9. Reading Connected Scatterplot Vectors: Up-Right ($\nearrow$) = Positive Correlation; Up-Left to Down-Right ($\searrow$) = Anticorrelation; Loops/Spirals = Cyclical phase-lagging cycles.
  10. The Biological Cause of the Keeling Cycle: The annual ~8 ppm dip in global atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ occurs during Northern Hemisphere summer because Earth's landmasses and terrestrial forests are overwhelmingly concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere, where massive seasonal photosynthesis draws down carbon.

Quick Flashcard Self-Drill Prompts

  • Q: What is LOWESS vs LOESS?  A: LOWESS is degree-1 (linear); LOESS is degree-2 (quadratic/parabolic).
  • Q: What is Eager vs Lazy learning?  A: Splines = Eager (solves once upfront, instant $O(1)$ lookup); LOESS = Lazy (fits WLS every query point).
  • Q: What are the 3 spline knot constraints?  A: $C^0$ equal value, $C^1$ equal slope, $C^2$ equal curvature.
  • Q: What does the slope of a Log-Log plot mean?  A: The exact power-law scaling exponent ($\alpha$).
