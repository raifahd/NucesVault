Textbook Reference Based on Fundamentals of Data Visualization by Claus O. Wilke (O'Reilly Media). High-yield exam notes tailored for DAV quizzes & MCQs.

1Handling Overlapping Points (Overplotting)

Chapter 18 • Overcoming visual occlusion in scatterplots through alpha blending, jittering, 2D histograms, and contour density estimation

When visualizing data, scatterplots are our primary tool for examining bivariate relationships between two continuous variables. However, standard scatterplots fail dramatically when points lie directly on top of one another. When multiple data marks occupy the identical spatial coordinates, they obscure one another—a phenomenon known as overplotting.

Definition
Overplotting: The visual occlusion that occurs when multiple data points in a scatterplot share identical or nearly identical coordinate positions, causing visual marks to overlap and hide the true volume and density distribution of the underlying data.
Intuitive One-Liner: A scatterplot illusion where 100 stacked points look exactly like a single point because they sit right on top of each other.

1. The Two Root Causes of Overplotting

Many analysts mistakenly assume overplotting only happens with "Big Data." Claus Wilke demonstrates that overplotting stems from two distinct mechanisms:

  • Large Sample Sizes ($N \gg 1,000$): When plotting hundreds of thousands of observations (e.g., all 100,000+ flight departures from Newark Airport in 2013), the plot canvas simply runs out of visual pixels. Even if coordinates are continuous, the sheer density turns the plot area into an illegible, solid black smudge.
  • Low Precision or Integer Rounding in Small Datasets: Even with small or moderate datasets ($N < 300$), if continuous quantities are rounded to discrete steps (e.g., engine displacement rounded to the nearest deciliter, or fuel efficiency rounded to integer miles per gallon), multiple completely distinct records collapse onto identical coordinate pairs.
Case Study • The 234 EPA Cars Dataset

Integer Rounding Occlusion (Wilke Figure 18-1)

Wilke analyzes city fuel economy versus engine displacement for 234 popular car models (1999–2008):

  • The Rounding Trap: Fuel economy is recorded in integer mpg; engine displacement is rounded to the nearest 0.1 liter.
  • The Hidden Population: There are 21 cars with a 2.0-liter engine. Yet because of rounding, those 21 cars produce only 4 distinct mpg values (19, 20, 21, or 22 mpg). As a result, 21 vehicles are represented by merely 4 visible dots! The 2.0-liter engine appears deceptively unpopular.
  • Total Data Occlusion: Two four-wheel drive (4WD) cars with 2.0-liter engines (encoded as black dots) are plotted underneath front-wheel drive (FWD) cars (yellow dots). The black dots are 100% occluded—giving the completely false impression that no 4WD 2.0L cars exist!

2. The Four Solutions: Principles, Mechanics & Trade-offs

Strategy 1: Partial Transparency (Alpha Blending)

By setting the alpha opacity of each glyph below 1.0 (e.g., alpha = 0.2), regions with multiple overlapping points build up deeper, darker shades of ink.

The Limits of Transparency (Exam Pitfall)
Why Alpha Blending Alone Is Not a Magic Bullet:
  1. Count Estimation Failure: While viewers can see that one point is darker than another, the human brain cannot reliably estimate whether a dark cluster represents 5 points, 20 points, or 100 points.
  2. Not Self-Explanatory: Casual readers seeing varying shades will wonder whether a third variable (like brightness or luminance) is being mapped, rather than realizing it represents overlapping dots.
  3. Dynamic Range Collapse: In massive datasets (e.g., Newark flight delays), no single alpha value works. If alpha is low enough to prevent high-density clusters (0–200 min delays) from becoming solid black blobs, the rare extreme outliers (400+ min delays) become completely invisible!

Strategy 2: Jittering

Jittering applies a small, randomized numerical perturbation to each point's $x$ and/or $y$ coordinate before rendering.

Definition
Jittering: The intentional addition of controlled random noise to discrete or rounded continuous coordinates to physically separate identical points and reveal stacking frequency.
Intuitive One-Liner: Giving identical points a gentle random shake so they don't cover each other up.
Critical Warning • The Danger of Over-Jittering
Jittering alters the underlying data! If you jitter too aggressively, you displace points far from their true coordinates, artificially blurring genuine boundaries and inventing non-existent relationships. Always keep jitter amplitude significantly smaller than the distance between adjacent measurement grid units.

Strategy 3: 2D Histograms (Rectangles vs. Hexagons)

When $N > 10,000$, plotting individual glyphs becomes computationally inefficient and visually futile. Instead, we partition the 2D plane into discrete bins, tally the number of observations falling into each bin, and encode bin counts via color/luminance.

Exam Pro Tip • Hexagon Binning Advantage (Carr et al. 1987)
Why are hexagons mathematically superior to squares/rectangles in 2D binning?
In a regular hexagon, every point inside the shape is on average closer to the hexagon's centroid than points in an equal-area square are to the square's center! Specifically:
  • A square has corners that stick far out from the center (ratio of corner distance to side distance is $\sqrt{2} pprox 1.414$).
  • A regular hexagon is much closer to a circle (ratio of vertex distance to apothem is $2/\sqrt{3} pprox 1.155$).
  • Consequently, assigning all points in the bin to the bin's centroid introduces lower spatial quantization error and less visual distortion than rectangular binning.

Strategy 4: 2D Density Contours & Small Multiples

Instead of discrete bins, 2D Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) computes a smooth continuous probability surface over $(x, y)$, which is then contoured using topological elevation lines (isodensity curves).

  • When it works: When point density varies slowly and smoothly across both the $x$ and $y$ dimensions (e.g., Blue Jays head length vs. body mass).
  • Shading Enhancement: Filling the areas between contour lines with progressively darker hues creates a powerful, intuitive impression of a rising 3D mountain of data density.
  • The Multi-Group Hairball Failure: Drawing colored contours for multiple subgroups works only for 2 to 3 clearly separated groups (e.g., male vs. female Blue Jays). In the diamonds dataset (53,940 diamonds with 5 cut qualities: Fair, Good, Very Good, Premium, Ideal), plotting 5 sets of contours on one panel produces an incomprehensible, overlapping "hairball" of crisscrossing lines.
  • The Faceting Solution: Split the multi-group contours into small multiples (facets), with one panel per cut grade, sharing an identical background coordinate grid so readers can visually calibrate locations across panels.
Real-World Finding • The Diamonds Paradox

Why Faceting Revealed Two Opposing Trends in the Diamonds Dataset

  1. Cut vs. Carat (Weight): Better cuts (Ideal, Premium) cluster at lower carat values than poorer cuts (Fair, Good). Why? Because jewelers must cut away and discard more raw diamond rock to achieve ideal facet geometry, yielding lighter stones on average!
  2. Cut vs. Price: Holding carat constant (e.g., looking exactly at 0.5 carats across panels), better cuts command substantially higher market prices.

High-Yield Practice Questions (Quiz Prep)
Question 1 (Easy): In a scatterplot of 234 cars, why did 21 vehicles with 2.0-liter engines appear as only 4 distinct dots?

Answer: Due to integer rounding and low measurement precision. Engine displacement was rounded to the nearest deciliter and fuel economy to integer mpg. The 21 cars only took on 4 discrete mpg values (19, 20, 21, and 22 mpg), collapsing 21 data points onto identical spatial coordinates.

Question 2 (Medium): Why is hexagonal binning mathematically preferable to rectangular binning according to Carr et al. (1987)?

Answer: Points inside a regular hexagon are, on average, closer to the hexagon's centroid than points inside an equal-area square/rectangle are to the square's center. This reduces spatial quantization distortion and provides a more accurate representation of local centroid density.

Question 3 (Hard): When plotting the 53,940 diamonds dataset across 5 cut categories, why did colored contour lines fail, and what was Wilke's solution?

Answer: Colored contour lines failed because having 5 overlapping, intermingled groups on a single panel creates a dense "hairball" of crisscrossing lines that obscures all group-specific distributions. Wilke solved this by using faceting (small multiples), plotting each cut's contours in its own separate panel with a shared background grid for visual calibration.

Key Takeaways • Overplotting (Chapter 18)

  • Overplotting is caused both by large datasets ($N > 10,000$) and by rounding/low precision in small datasets.
  • Partial transparency highlights density variations but fails when density ranges span multiple orders of magnitude.
  • Jittering exposes hidden stacked points but directly modifies true coordinates; use with extreme restraint.
  • Hexagonal binning provides superior geometric accuracy over square binning because hexagon points are closer to their centroid.
  • When multi-group contour plots turn into "hairballs," split them into faceted small multiples with a shared reference grid.

2Common Pitfalls of Color Use

Chapter 19 • Encoding limits, direct labeling, saturation fatigue, nonmonotonic rainbow dangers, and CVD design rules

Color is one of the most expressive visual aesthetic channels available to a data scientist. When deployed thoughtfully, it directs viewer attention, clarifies grouping, and communicates continuous magnitude. However, poor color choices can instantly destroy an otherwise mathematically sound visualization.

Core Design Axiom
The Wilke Principle of Color: Color must always serve a specific functional purpose, must be unambiguous and perceptually uniform, and must never distract or fatigue the reader.
Intuitive One-Liner: If a color doesn't convey vital data or structure, it is visual clutter.

1. Pitfall 1 • Encoding Too Much or Irrelevant Information

A frequent error in data presentation is giving color a job that is far too large for human visual cognition to handle.

  • The 50-State Legend Puzzle (Figure 19-1): Attempting to assign 50 distinct colors to all 50 US states plus DC creates an infuriating visual puzzle. Many colors end up nearly indistinguishable, forcing the reader into exhausting mental ping-pong between the legend and scatter dots.
  • The Cognitive Limit of Qualitative Color: As a strict empirical rule of thumb, qualitative color scales work best for 3 to 5 distinct categories. Once you reach 8 to 10 categories, matching colors to categories becomes burdensome. Above 10, human color memory and discrimination fail entirely.
  • The Direct Labeling Remedy: When distinguishing more than about 8 categorical items, abandon color coding and use direct text labeling instead. You do not need to label all 50 items—label a meaningful representative subset (e.g., states with extreme growth or outliers discussed in the text) and provide the full dataset as an accompanying table.
  • Gratuitous Rainbow Coloring (Figure 19-3): Giving every bar in a bar plot its own distinct color without any data-mapping rationale creates a meaningless "rainbow effect." It adds zero information while actively distracting the reader's eye.

2. Pitfall 2 • Overly Saturated Colors

Large filled areas of pure, highly saturated colors create extreme visual contrast that fatigues the human eye (visual glare).

Design Guideline • Avoid High Saturation in Large Fills
Why Over-Saturation Ruins Figures:
  • High-saturation blocks dominate the visual field, drawing the reader's eyes aggressively away from text labels and numerical values.
  • Strongly saturated bars make it physically uncomfortable to align bar endpoints with subtle underlying grid lines.
  • Best Practice: Use muted, desaturated tones for large fills, reserving vibrant, saturated colors exclusively for small accent marks, outlier dots, or focal lines.

Native Color Gradient Comparison • Rainbow Flaw vs. Perceptually Monotonic Scales

Demonstration of why the popular rainbow scale is scientifically defective compared to monotonic sequential and diverging scales.

✗ Rainbow Scale (Highly Nonmonotonic, Circular & Flawed) Bad Default

Starts and ends with dark red (circular). Wide yellowish plateau occupies ~33% of scale where luminance barely changes, followed by rapid drops in dark blue.

✓ Sequential Heat Scale (Monotonic Luminance Gradient) CVD-Safe Sequential

Smooth, continuous progression from dark red to light yellow. In grayscale or under any CVD, magnitude correlates directly with perceived brightness.

✓ ColorBrewer PiYG (CVD-Safe Diverging Scale: Pink to Yellow-Green) CVD-Safe Diverging

Safe alternative to red-green. Pink contains blue (discernible by deutans/protans); yellow-green contains yellow (discernible by tritans).

3. Pitfall 3 • Nonmonotonic Color Scales (The Rainbow Trap)

In Chapter 4 and Chapter 19, Wilke highlights the two non-negotiable mathematical requirements for sequential color scales:

  1. Colors must clearly convey which values are larger or smaller than others.
  2. The perceptual difference between two colors must faithfully reflect the numerical difference between the underlying data values.

The ubiquitous rainbow color scale (jet) violates both principles:

  • Circularity: It spans the entire visible electromagnetic spectrum, starting at dark red and ending near dark red/purple. Two extreme opposite values look nearly identical!
  • Nonmonotonic Luminance: When converted to grayscale, the rainbow scale goes from medium-dark $ ightarrow$ bright $ ightarrow$ very dark $ ightarrow$ medium-dark. Brightness fluctuates wildly rather than scaling monotonically with data values.
  • Artificial Feature Creation (Figure 19-5): In the Texas census map, the rainbow scale places intense, sharp visual boundaries around counties with ~75% white population, making an arbitrary continuous threshold look like a dramatic sociological cliff!

4. Pitfall 4 • Not Designing for Color-Vision Deficiency (CVD)

Approximately 8% of males and 0.5% of females of Northern European ancestry experience some form of color-vision deficiency. If your visualization relies on fragile color pairs, nearly 1 in 10 male readers will be unable to read it.

CVD Classification Receptor Defect Affected Cones Prevalence & Clinical Impact Confused Color Pairs

How Different Scales Behave Under CVD:

  • Sequential Scales: Inherently safe as long as they follow a monotonic lightness gradient (e.g., dark red to pale yellow). Under CVD, colors shift hue but lightness differences remain crystal clear.
  • Diverging Scales: Traditional red-versus-green contrasts completely fail for 99% of CVD readers. Use ColorBrewer PiYG (Pink-to-Yellow-Green) or Blue-to-Orange instead.
  • Qualitative Scales: The hardest challenge because multiple discrete colors must remain distinguishable under all simulations.

Crucial Research Finding • Graphical Element Size (Stone et al. 2014)
Why CVD-Safe Palettes Can Still Fail on Small Elements:
Maureen Stone, Danielle Albers Szafir, and Vidya Setlur (2014) demonstrated that human color discrimination is fundamentally dependent on spatial size. Colors are drastically easier to differentiate in large, contiguous regions (e.g., thick bars or polygons) than in tiny scatter dots or razor-thin lines. Under CVD, this degradation is severe.
The Golden Rule: Never rely on palette formulas alone. Always test your final figure in a CVD simulator (or desaturate to grayscale) at its actual printed size!
High-Yield Practice Questions (Quiz Prep)
Question 1 (Easy): What is the clinical difference between terms ending in "-anomaly" (e.g. deuteranomaly) and "-anopia" (e.g. deuteranopia)?

Answer: Terms ending in "-anomaly" refer to partial impairment or anomalous trichromacy (the cone pigment is mutated or shifted in spectral sensitivity). Terms ending in "-anopia" refer to the complete absence of the respective cone photoreceptor (dichromacy).

Question 2 (Medium): Why does the ColorBrewer PiYG (pink to yellow-green) scale remain distinguishable to individuals with red-green color-vision deficiency?

Answer: Because the reddish end is not a pure red but a pink (which contains a significant blue component), while the greenish end is a yellow-green (which contains significant yellow/red). People with red-green CVD (deutans and protans) can easily detect the variation in the blue component, while tritans detect the variation in the red component.

Question 3 (Hard): What are the two fundamental conditions for sequential color scales, and how does the rainbow scale violate them?

Answer: (1) The colors must clearly indicate which values are larger or smaller (order); (2) differences between colors must faithfully reflect differences in numerical values. The rainbow scale violates both: it is circular (starts and ends with dark red), has nonmonotonic luminance (brightness rises and dips erratically in grayscale), and exhibits nonuniform rates of change (a huge yellow/green plateau followed by steep blue drops).

Key Takeaways • Pitfalls of Color (Chapter 19)

  • Qualitative colors should be restricted to 3–5 categories; beyond ~8 categories, use direct text labeling.
  • Avoid large filled regions of overly saturated colors; they produce visual fatigue and overpower data.
  • The rainbow color scale is circular, nonmonotonic in luminance, and introduces false boundaries.
  • Approximately 8% of males have CVD; deuteranomaly (green-weak) is the most prevalent form.
  • The Okabe & Ito 8-color palette provides a mathematically proven CVD-safe standard for categorical data.
  • Color perception degrades rapidly on tiny dots or thin lines (Stone et al. 2014); always verify in a CVD simulator.

3Redundant Coding & Legend Design

Chapter 20 • Multichannel aesthetic mapping, visual order matching, direct labeling, the two-shades rule, and axis colorbars

In Chapter 19, we saw that relying solely on color to convey categorical distinctions frequently fails. When elements are small, when viewers suffer from color-vision deficiency, or when a document is printed in grayscale, color alone is insufficient. The universal antidote to this fragility is redundant coding.

Definition
Redundant Coding: The deliberate visualization practice of encoding the same underlying data variable across two or more distinct aesthetic channels simultaneously (such as color plus shape, or color plus line type, or color plus position).
Intuitive One-Liner: Giving data a "backup channel" so if color fails, shape or position still delivers the message.

1. Designing Legends with Redundant Coding

The canonical failure of single-channel coding is demonstrated in Fisher's Iris dataset (Sepal Width vs. Sepal Length for Iris setosa, Iris virginica, and Iris versicolor):

  • The Single-Channel Failure (Figure 20-1): When species is encoded only by dot color (yellow, green, blue), the intermingling points of virginica and versicolor are hard to separate. In black-and-white printing or under blue-yellow CVD (tritanomaly), green and blue become indistinguishable. In pure grayscale, all three species merge into an unreadable mess!
  • The Redundant Solution (Figures 20-3 & 20-4):
    1. Swap color assignments so adjacent/overlapping groups don't use close hues.
    2. Assign different geometric shapes: e.g., solid circles for setosa, solid triangles for virginica, and solid squares for versicolor.
    Even when 100% desaturated to pure grayscale, every individual data point remains instantly identifiable by its glyph geometry.

Native Visual Comparison • Default Alphabetical Legend vs. Visual Order Matching vs. Direct Labeling

How cognitive lookup friction is eliminated by ordering legends to match data curves, or bypassing legends completely through direct labels.

A. Default Alphabetical Legend High Cognitive Load

Alphabetical sorting forces viewer's gaze to jump up and down across legend items to find the matching curve.

B. Matched Visual Order Low Mental Effort

Legend order strictly matches vertical line endpoints (Facebook top, Apple bottom). Even in grayscale, positions align.

C. Direct Labeling (No Legend) Zero Split Attention

Labels placed directly adjacent to curve endpoints. Eliminates legend box entirely, freeing canvas space.

2. The Visual Order Matching Rule

Most modern plotting libraries (e.g., ggplot2, matplotlib, seaborn) generate legends automatically by sorting factor levels alphabetically. Wilke emphasizes that this automated behavior is a subtle yet pervasive flaw:

The Golden Rule of Legend Ordering (Exam Essential)
If there is a visual ordering in your data, make sure to match it in the legend!
In the 4 major tech stocks plot (Figure 20-5):
  • The four stock curves end at clearly differentiated vertical heights: Facebook is highest, followed by Alphabet, Microsoft, and Apple at the bottom.
  • The default software legend sorts them alphabetically: Alphabet, Apple, Facebook, Microsoft.
  • To interpret the graph, the viewer must perform an exhausting series of mental saccades, linking top-to-bottom curves with an out-of-order legend.
  • When Wilke reorders the legend to: Facebook, Alphabet, Microsoft, Apple, the cognitive load drops to near zero!
  • CVD & Grayscale Superpower: Under colorblindness or grayscale printouts where hues look identical, matching the order allows the viewer to reliably match lines to companies based purely on vertical position!

3. Designing Figures Without Legends (Direct Labeling)

Legends always impose an unavoidable cognitive friction called the Split-Attention Effect: the reader's working memory must store a color or symbol from one corner of the plot and transport it across the canvas to decode a curve.

Definition
Direct Labeling: The design technique of placing explanatory text labels directly adjacent to the graphical marks they describe (e.g., at line endpoints or inside clusters), completely eliminating the need for an external legend box.
Intuitive One-Liner: Writing the label right where the data lives instead of hiding it in an external lookup box.

Three Direct Labeling Techniques:

  1. Line Curves: Place the label directly at the rightmost terminal point of each curve (Figure 20-8).
  2. Scatterplot Clusters: Draw faint enclosing ellipses around distinct point clouds and position text labels directly adjacent to the ellipse perimeter (Figure 20-9).
  3. Marginal Density Plots: Place 1D density curves in the upper and right margins of the scatterplot and direct-label those marginal curves (Figure 20-11). This keeps the central scatterplot uncluttered while completely replacing the legend.
Wilke's Pro Trick • The "Two Shades" Rule for Colored Text

Why Colored Text Often Looks Faint, and How to Fix It

When applying direct labels, authors often color the text label with the exact same RGB color used for the filled data shapes. However:

  • The Optical Thin-Line Illusion: Text glyphs consist of razor-thin strokes. Even if printed in the identical hex code as an adjacent filled circle, the text will appear significantly lighter and washed out to the human eye.
  • The Solution: Use two different shades of each color hue:
    • A lighter shade for solid filled regions, polygons, or point bodies.
    • A darker shade of the same hue for thin lines, borders, and direct text labels!

4. Integrated Axis Colorbars

When a single continuous variable is mapped onto both a spatial axis position and a color gradient (e.g., daily temperature mapped to $x$-position and point color), you should never provide a separate floating legend box.
Instead, integrate the reference color bar directly along and into the primary axis (Wilke Figure 20-12). This visually fuses position and color into a single unified scale.

High-Yield Practice Questions (Quiz Prep)
Question 1 (Easy): What is redundant coding, and why is it recommended for categorical scatterplots?

Answer: Redundant coding is mapping a single data variable onto multiple aesthetic dimensions at once (e.g., color + point shape). In categorical scatterplots, it ensures that if color discrimination is impaired (by CVD, washed-out projectors, or grayscale photocopying), distinct symbol shapes allow readers to identify categories without loss of information.

Question 2 (Medium): In a multi-line time series plot where curves end at different heights, why should the legend NOT be ordered alphabetically?

Answer: Because alphabetical ordering ignores the visual hierarchy of the data lines. Forcing the reader to jump back and forth between alphabetical names and vertically ranked curves creates high cognitive friction. Ordering the legend to match the vertical rank of the line endpoints enables immediate mental alignment, even under color vision deficiency.

Question 3 (Hard): Explain Wilke's "Two Shades" rule for direct-labeled figures. What perceptual problem does it resolve?

Answer: Text characters consist of very thin strokes, which human vision perceives as lighter and less saturated than a large filled polygon of the exact same color. To ensure legibility without mismatch, Wilke recommends using a lighter shade for filled shapes and a darker shade of the same hue for thin lines, borders, and text labels.

Key Takeaways • Redundant Coding (Chapter 20)

  • Always combine color with distinct point shapes in scatterplots to guarantee grayscale and CVD legibility.
  • Match the legend order to the perceived vertical ordering of data lines; never accept default alphabetical sorting.
  • Direct labeling eliminates the cognitive split-attention effect caused by detached legend boxes.
  • Apply the "Two Shades" rule: lighter tints for filled areas, darker shades of the same hue for lines and text.
  • Integrate continuous color legends directly into their corresponding spatial axis instead of using floating boxes.

4Balance the Data and the Context

Chapter 23 • Non-data ink, background grids, the ggplot2 debate, minimal grid orientation, white gridlines, and paired data reference lines

A common debate in data visualization centers on non-data ink—the visual marks that do not directly represent numerical data, such as axis lines, ticks, background grids, and frames. Edward Tufte famously advocated maximizing the "data-ink ratio" by stripping away non-data marks. However, Claus Wilke shows that over-erasing non-data ink is just as destructive as over-cluttering.

The Goldilocks Principle of Context
Data-Context Balance: Figure design must occupy the optimal middle ground: the actual data must stand out as the undisputed visual hero, but accompanied by just enough contextual scaffolding (grids, tick labels, reference baselines) to anchor values and enable quantitative judgment.
Intuitive One-Liner: Too much scaffolding hides the building; too little scaffolding makes the building collapse into thin air.

1. The Two Harmful Extremes

Native Visual Comparison • Non-Data Ink Spectrum

Demonstration of how excessive ink creates visual noise, extreme minimalism creates unanchored floating marks, and balanced design achieves harmony.

A. Excessive Ink (Figure 23-1) Heavy Clutter

Unnecessary outer frames, nested legend borders, and prominent dark gridlines overpower the data points.

B. Over-Erased Ink (Figure 23-3) Floating Marks

Points float in empty void without baseline or reference. Faint legend dot is easily mistaken for a data point!

C. Balanced Context (Figure 23-2) Optimal Harmony

Light gray major grid anchors points in space. Frames removed, high-contrast labels, data marks are undisputed heroes.

2. The Background Grid Debate (The ggplot2 Style)

Hadley Wickham popularized the signature ggplot2 default: a light gray plot background covered by white major and minor grid lines (Figure 23-7).

Wickham vs. Wilke • The Scientific Debate
Arguments in Favor (Hadley Wickham):
  1. Single Visual Entity: The continuous gray canvas binds the entire plot area together as a unified visual shape.
  2. Preventing the "White Box" Illusion: In dense academic typesetting (e.g., 10-point Times New Roman with tight leading), a bright white plot panel can look like a jarring, hollow white hole punched into dark text. A subtle gray background softens this contrast.
Wilke's Critique & Cautions:
  • Data Attenuation: The gray background reduces the luminance contrast between the background and data ink, often overpowering delicate lines or light colors.
  • Legend Confusion: Gray background squares carried over into legend key swatches create unnecessary visual distraction.
  • Typography Relativity: The darkness of surrounding body text varies wildly between documents (e.g., a 14pt coffee-table book is much lighter than 10pt paper). If the surrounding page is airy, a gray plot background ends up standing out as an intrusive, dark box!

3. Minimal Grid Orientation: The Perpendicular Rule

When designing custom or minimal grid lines, you should not mindlessly draw a 2D checkerboard across both axes. Instead, align your grid lines with human perceptual needs:

The Perpendicular Grid Rule
The Orthogonal Alignment Principle: Grid lines are most informative when drawn perpendicular (orthogonal) to the primary variable of interest along which numbers vary.
Intuitive One-Liner: If the data goes up and down, run grid lines left and right. If the bars go left and right, run grid lines up and down.
  • Time Series & Trend Curves (Figure 23-10): The primary quantity of interest is the change in the vertical $y$-variable over time. Therefore, draw thin horizontal grid lines at major $y$-axis ticks. Omit vertical grid lines entirely! The evenly spaced horizontal lines simultaneously mark the extent of the panel, eliminating the need for heavy axis lines.
  • Horizontal Bar Charts (Figure 23-11): Because the bars extend horizontally along $x$, readers compare horizontal bar lengths. Therefore, draw thin vertical grid lines to provide precise numerical references across bars. Omit horizontal grid lines!
Case Study • Indexed Stock Price Context

The Power of a Single Reference Line (Figure 23-9)

When plotting tech stock prices normalized to 100 in June 2012:

  • Leaving the plot area completely empty (Figure 23-8) makes it difficult to ascertain whether a curve has deviated from baseline, especially years later at the far right edge.
  • Adding a single thin horizontal reference line at $y = 100$ provides an instant anchor that establishes baseline parity across the entire 5-year timeline.

4. White Grid Lines on Bars & The Labeling Trap

Edward Tufte (2001) proposed drawing thin white grid lines on top of dark bar graphs instead of drawing dark lines behind them (Figure 23-12).

  • The Perceptual Benefit: Empirical studies (Haroz, Kosara, & Franconeri 2015) confirm that segmenting bars into discrete visual units helps human readers judge bar lengths more accurately.
  • The Aesthetic Downside: White lines can create the optical illusion that the bars are "falling apart" into disjointed blocks rather than forming a solid unit.
  • The Visual Elongation Trap: If value labels do not fit inside the bar segments, moving labels outside the ends of the bars visually elongates the bars, misleading the reader about their true length. Keep value labels inside the bars or rely on the grid!

5. Paired Data: Why a Diagonal Line ($x = y$) Beats a Background Grid

In scatterplots of paired experimental data (e.g., Gene expression in mutant bacteriophage T7 vs. wild-type bacteriophage, Paff et al. 2018):

Exam Trap • Paired Data Visual Reference
Never use a background grid as the primary reference for paired data!
  • In paired comparisons, the central research question is whether $y > x$ (upregulated) or $y < x$ (downregulated).
  • When plotted against a standard background grid (Figure 23-14), viewers cannot reliably determine which genes deviate from equality. Specifically, gene 10A, which suffered a major expression drop in the mutant virus, completely fails to stand out against a grid!
  • When plotted with a 45-degree diagonal reference line ($x = y$) and no distracting grid (Figure 23-13), genes 8, 9, and 10A immediately leap out as significant outliers lying far below the diagonal.
High-Yield Practice Questions (Quiz Prep)
Question 1 (Easy): What is the Perpendicular Grid Rule, and how does it apply to horizontal bar charts?

Answer: The Perpendicular Grid Rule states that grid lines should run orthogonal to the direction along which the variable of interest varies. For horizontal bar charts, bar lengths vary along the horizontal $x$-axis, so grid lines should be drawn vertically to facilitate visual comparison of bar endpoints. Horizontal lines should be omitted.

Question 2 (Medium): Why does Wilke argue that drawing a 45-degree diagonal line ($x = y$) is superior to a background grid when analyzing paired gene expression data?

Answer: Paired comparisons specifically test for deviation from equality ($x = y$). A 45-degree diagonal line immediately separates upregulated genes (above the line) from downregulated genes (below the line). A background grid lacks this angular reference, obscuring critical outliers (such as gene 10A in the bacteriophage T7 dataset).

Question 3 (Hard): What are the two main arguments in favor of ggplot2's light gray background with white gridlines, and what are Wilke's counter-arguments?

Answer: In favor: (1) It unifies the plot area into a single cohesive visual object; (2) it prevents a blinding "white box" illusion when set inside dense, dark body text. Wilke's counter-arguments: The gray background lowers contrast with data marks, clutters legend swatches with gray squares, and if surrounding text is light or airy, the plot stands out as an unwanted dark box.

Key Takeaways • Balancing Data & Context (Chapter 23)

  • Avoid both extreme ink overload (heavy borders, dark grids) and excessive minimalism (unanchored floating dots).
  • Grid lines should run perpendicular to the primary dimension of interest (horizontal lines for time series; vertical lines for bar charts).
  • A single reference line (such as $y = 100$ for indexed series) often delivers more clarity than an entire checkerboard grid.
  • Placing value labels outside the ends of bars improperly elongates their perceived length.
  • For paired data comparisons, always prioritize a 45-degree diagonal line ($x = y$) over background grids.

5Typography & Sizing: Use Larger Axis Labels

Chapter 24 • The universal default affliction, balanced visual scaling, the Monitor Bias Trap, and the scaling-down rule

In the opening lines of Chapter 24, Claus Wilke delivers the most emphatic piece of advice in his entire book: "If you take away only one single lesson from this book, make it this one: pay attention to your axis labels, axis tick labels, and other assorted plot annotations. Chances are they are too small!"

The Number One Takeaway
The Default Axis Label Affliction: Nearly all graphing libraries and software platforms (R, Python, Excel, MATLAB) possess universally poor typography defaults. If you accept the default font sizes without manual overrides, your labels will almost certainly be illegible in final publications, presentations, and reports.
Intuitive One-Liner: Software defaults are designed for editing on giant screens, not for humans reading final reports.

1. The Visual Progression of Label Scaling (Figures 24-1 to 24-5)

Wilke demonstrates the impact of label scaling using the Australian male athletes dataset (Percent Body Fat vs. Height):

  • Figure 24-1 ("Bad" • Default Size): Microscopic axis titles, illegible tick marks, and tiny legend text. Viewers must physically zoom in or squint to read variable names.
  • Figure 24-2 ("Ugly" • Slight Increase): Text is somewhat larger and barely legible, but the overall figure remains visibly unbalanced—the text elements feel frail and disconnected from the plot canvas.
  • Figure 24-3 ("Good" • Full-Page Target): Harmonious balance between text typography and data marks when reproduced as a full-page figure.
  • Figure 24-4 ("Bad / Unbalanced" • Disproportionate Enlargement): The author enlarges text labels, but leaves data points as tiny dots. This produces severe visual dissonance: heavy, bold text overpowers frail, tiny data marks.
  • Figure 24-5 ("Good" • Balanced for Scaling Down): Both text labels and data points are proportionally enlarged. On a massive computer monitor, this figure might feel exaggerated, but when scaled down to a standard 2-to-3-inch journal column or mobile screen, it is the ONLY figure in the chapter that remains crisp, balanced, and readable!

Native Visual Demonstration • The Scaling Down Reality

Why figures that look "just fine" on a 27-inch 4K monitor become unreadable disasters in a 3-inch journal column.

A. Designer's Screen (Large Canvas) Deceptive Legibility

On a 27-inch 4K monitor, tiny 8pt font looks readable because the physical canvas is 15 inches wide.

B. Print / Slide Reality (Scaled to 3") Illegible Disaster

Scaled down for print columns, slides, or smartphones, default labels vanish into unreadable scratches.

2. The Psychological "Monitor Bias Trap"

Why do experienced researchers and engineers consistently generate figures with microscopic text? Wilke identifies a psychological cognitive bias rooted in modern computer hardware:

Psychological Phenomenon
The Monitor Bias Trap: Analysts design and preview figures on large, high-resolution desktop displays where the graphic occupies 500 to 1,000 square centimeters. In this expanded viewing state, small labels appear sharp and readable, while properly sized labels feel awkward, cartoonish, or oversized.
Intuitive One-Liner: You are designing for a billboard while your reader is viewing on a postcard.

3. The Golden Rule of Sizing & Verification

The Practical Print-Size Verification Test (Exam Rule)
Always look at scaled-down versions of your figures to make sure the axis labels are appropriately sized!
Before finalizing any figure for an exam, thesis, paper, or slide presentation:
  1. The 3-Inch Zoom Test: Zoom out on your screen until the figure occupies only 3 to 5 inches (7 to 12 cm) in width. Can you still effortlessly read every tick number, axis title, and legend note without leaning forward?
  2. The 6-Foot Step-Back Test: Step back 6 feet (2 meters) from your desktop monitor. If the axis titles blur into gray lines, increase the base font size immediately!
  3. Proportional Glyph Scaling: If you increase font size, you must proportionally increase point sizes and line widths to preserve visual balance.
High-Yield Practice Questions (Quiz Prep)
Question 1 (Easy): What is Claus Wilke's single most important piece of advice across the entire textbook?

Answer: Pay close attention to your axis labels, axis tick labels, and plot annotations—they are almost certainly too small because default software settings are universally poor.

Question 2 (Medium): What psychological reason does Wilke provide for why data scientists routinely make axis labels too small?

Answer: The Monitor Bias Trap: analysts preview graphics at full-screen size on large, high-resolution desktop monitors. At that massive size, small text appears perfectly crisp and readable, whereas appropriately scaled text feels artificially large. When the graphic is later scaled down for publication, the text becomes unreadable.

Question 3 (Hard): In Wilke Figure 24-4, the author increased the label fonts substantially, yet the figure was still labeled "bad." Why, and how did Figure 24-5 fix it?

Answer: Figure 24-4 was bad because it was visually unbalanced: the text was enlarged, but the data points remained tiny dots, creating severe visual dissonance where text overpowered data. Key Takeaways • Larger Axis Labels (Chapter 24)

  • Software defaults for axis labels and annotations are almost always too small.
  • Always scale text elements proportionally with data marks to maintain visual balance.
  • Beware the Monitor Bias Trap: previewing at full-screen hides scaling defects.
  • Always test figures at their final reproduction scale (zoom out to 3 inches or step back 6 feet).
  • A figure that looks slightly oversized on a desktop monitor will look perfect in print.

6Avoid Line Drawings: Solid Shapes vs. Outlines

Chapter 25 • Historical relics of manual drafting, perceptual superiority of solid shapes, and 4 classic plot comparisons

Throughout modern data visualization, a surprising number of plots are still drawn using hollow outlines, empty boxes, dashed curves, and open glyphs. Claus Wilke establishes a firm design rule: visualize data with solid, colored shapes rather than with lines that merely outline those shapes.

Core Design Principle
The Solid Shape Principle: Human visual cognition perceives solid filled shapes as coherent physical objects. Solid shapes immediately convey quantitative volume and density, eliminate Moiré optical illusions, and possess superior visual presence compared to empty line drawings.
Critical Constraint: This guideline does not supersede the Principle of Proportional Ink (the visual area of filled ink must always be proportional to the data values represented).

1. Historical Origin: Why Line Drawings Persist

Why do modern software packages still default to hollow lines and empty shapes?

  • 20th-Century Manual Drafting Constraints: For over a century, scientific figures were drawn by hand using technical pens (Rapidograph) or early mechanical pen plotters. Visualizations had to be reproduced on black-and-white printing presses.
  • The Hatching Workaround: Because solid fills or continuous grayscale gradients were technically impossible or cost-prohibitive, draftsmen simulated shading by drawing hatch lines, cross-hatching, stippling, and broken/dashed outlines.
  • Software Inertia: Early plotting libraries written in the 1970s and 1980s mimicked mechanical pen plotters. Today, even though modern displays and digital publishing support millions of colors and alpha transparency, legacy default styles linger.

Native Visual Comparison • Empty Line Drawings vs. Solid Filled Shapes

Demonstration of how empty outlines create ambiguous boundaries and optical noise, while solid fills create clear, coherent objects.

1. Histogram: Empty vs. Filled Empty = Ambiguous

Empty bars leave ambiguity where gaps exist. Filled bars instantly reveal the distribution shape.

2. Density: Dashed vs. Shaded Dashed = Porous

Dashed curves look porous and intersect into a messy hairball. Transparent shading clearly bounds density.

3. Scatter: Open vs. Solid Solid Glyphs Win

Open glyphs generate visual noise. Solid glyphs stand out with high visual weight and distinct silhouettes.

4. Boxplot: Empty vs. Shaded Anchored IQR

Empty boxes blend into background gaps. Light shading anchors the middle 50% without visual ambiguity.

2. Four Classic Plot Comparisons (Outlines vs. Solid Shapes)

1. Histograms and Bar Plots (Figures 25-1 & 25-2)

When histogram bars are drawn as empty wireframes, readers experience immediate spatial ambiguity:

  • The Inside/Outside Dilemma: In sparse bins or gaps between bars, it is not visually evident which side of a vertical wire is "inside" a bar and which side is empty background.
  • The Remedy: Fill bars with a solid neutral tint (e.g., light gray, muted blue, or terracotta). The distribution shape becomes instantly readable.

2. Density Estimates (Figures 25-3 to 25-5)

Old-school line density plots (Figure 25-3) exhibit two fatal flaws:

  1. The Porous Boundary Defect: Dashed or dotted line styles look porous; they fail to form an enclosed visual container between the area under the curve and the empty sky above it.
  2. Intersection Hairball: When multiple density curves overlap, unshaded wireframe lines create confusing topological loops where viewers cannot trace individual species distributions.
  3. The Solution: Partially transparent filled areas (alpha ≈ 0.4) that allow overlapping probability distributions to shine through while perceiving each as a distinct, solid object.

3. Scatterplots: The "Open Point" Fallacy (Figures 25-6 & 25-7)

Many researchers argue that open points (circles, squares, crosses) are superior because their hollow centers allow you to see points underneath. Wilke completely rejects this claim:

Exam Trap • The Open Point Myth
Why open points are NOT an acceptable solution for overplotting:
  • Open glyphs produce severe visual clutter and optical vibration (noise).
  • They lack visual presence and their boundaries blur into adjacent marks.
  • Wilke's Verdict: The minor advantage of seeing underlying marks does NOT justify the huge visual noise penalty. If you have overplotting, use proper Chapter 18 solutions (alpha blending, jittering, or 2D binning) and keep your symbols solid!

4. Boxplots: Shading the Middle 50% (Figures 25-8 & 25-9)

Traditional boxplots draw empty white rectangles. In a dense plot of 12 monthly distributions (Lincoln, NE temperatures):

  • Empty boxes and whisker lines create optical illusions where negative space between boxes is mistaken for boxes.
  • Giving the box a light gray fill sets it off cleanly against the canvas.
  • Refuting the Critique: Critics sometimes claim that shading the box gives "too much visual weight to the middle 50% of the data." Wilke responds: It is the inherent definition and purpose of a boxplot to highlight the interquartile range (IQR)! If you do not want to emphasize the center 50%, do not use a boxplot—use a violin plot, jittered points, or a sina plot!
High-Yield Practice Questions (Quiz Prep)
Question 1 (Easy): What is the historical reason why line drawings and hatched patterns were so widely used in scientific visualization?

Answer: Figures were drafted by hand with technical pens or early pen plotters for black-and-white printing presses. Solid fills and grayscale printing were technically impossible or cost-prohibitive, forcing draftsmen to simulate shading through hatching, stippling, and line outlines.

Question 2 (Medium): Why does Wilke reject the argument that "open points" (hollow circles/crosses) should be used in scatterplots to handle overplotting?

Answer: Because the severe visual noise, optical clutter, and lack of visual weight caused by open symbols far outweigh any minor benefit of seeing underlying points. Overplotting should instead be solved using dedicated techniques (such as alpha blending, jittering, or 2D hexagonal binning) while keeping point glyphs solid.

Question 3 (Hard): How does Wilke rebut the criticism that shading the inside of a boxplot gives "too much visual weight to the center 50% of the data"?

Answer: He explains that the fundamental mathematical intent of a boxplot is specifically to highlight the interquartile range (the middle 50%). If a researcher does not wish to emphasize the center 50%, the correct solution is not to draw empty boxes, but rather to use an entirely different visualization geometry—such as a violin plot, a sina plot, or jittered raw data points.

Key Takeaways • Avoid Line Drawings (Chapter 25)

  • Always visualize data with solid, colored shapes rather than empty outlines.
  • Solid shapes are perceived as coherent objects and convey quantitative volume.
  • Histograms with empty wireframe bars create inside/outside ambiguity; always use filled bars.
  • Dashed density lines look porous; use partially transparent shaded density polygons.
  • Avoid open scatterplot glyphs; their visual noise ruins legibility.
  • Shade the boxes of boxplots with light gray to anchor the interquartile range.

2. The Top 10 "Must-Know" Exam Pitfalls & Traps

High-Yield Exam Distractor Alerts
  1. Trap 1 (Overplotting in small data): Overplotting occurs in small datasets whenever measurements are recorded with low precision or rounded to integers (e.g. 234 cars, 21 with 2.0L engines).
  2. Trap 2 (Hexagon mathematical advantage): Hexagonal binning is superior to square binning because points inside a regular hexagon are, on average, closer to the center than points in an equal-area square (Carr et al. 1987).
  3. Trap 3 (Rainbow scale circularity): The rainbow scale is circular because its start and end colors are both shades of dark red, meaning the two most distant data values look almost identical.
  4. Trap 4 (CVD demographics): Approximately 8% of males and 0.5% of females have CVD. Deuteranomaly (green-weak) is the most common form; tritanomaly (blue-weak) is rare.
  5. Trap 5 (Why PiYG is CVD-safe): ColorBrewer PiYG works because pink contains a strong blue component (distinguished by deutans/protans) and yellow-green contains yellow (distinguished by tritans).
  6. Trap 6 (The "Two Shades" Rule): Text characters are thin lines and appear lighter than solid fills of the same color; fix this by using a lighter shade for fills and a darker shade of the same hue for lines and text labels.
  7. Trap 7 (Legend visual order): Legends should never default to alphabetical order if curves have a clear vertical visual rank; ordering the legend to match the curve endpoints drastically cuts cognitive load and aids CVD readers.
  8. Trap 8 (Perpendicular Grid Rule): For vertical fluctuations (time series), draw horizontal lines; for horizontal bar charts, draw vertical lines. Never draw arbitrary 2D checkerboards when one dimension dominates.
  9. Trap 9 (Paired data $x = y$ line): A 45-degree diagonal line is vastly superior to a background grid for paired data (e.g., mutant vs wild-type virus genes) because it immediately separates $y > x$ from $y < x$.
  10. Trap 10 (Open points myth): Open points (hollow circles/crosses) add visual noise and do NOT solve overplotting; use solid shapes with alpha blending, jittering, or binning.
