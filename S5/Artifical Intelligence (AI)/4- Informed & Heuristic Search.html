Informed & Heuristic Search

1The Nature of Heuristics

Harnessing domain knowledge to conquer combinatorial explosion

In the previous lectures, we studied blind (uninformed) search methods such as BFS, DFS, and UCS. They are termed "blind" because they possess zero insight into whether one unexpanded node is physically closer to the goal than another. They trudge through the graph purely by mechanical expansion.

In contrast, Informed (Heuristic) Search leverages domain-specific information (rules of thumb, estimates, mathematical metrics) to evaluate candidate states and prioritize searching along the most promising branches.

Definition • George F. Luger
Heuristic: A rule of thumb, strategy, trick, simplification, or any other kind of device which drastically limits search for solutions in large problem spaces. A heuristic is an informed guess of the next step to be taken in solving a problem, often based on experience, intuition, or simplified models.

The Two Fundamental Reasons Why Heuristics Are Used

According to Luger (Chapter 4.0) and Lecture 7 slides, heuristics are indispensable in artificial intelligence for two distinct reasons:

  1. Inherent Ambiguity in the Problem Statement or Available Data:

    The problem may not admit an exact, closed-form algorithmic solution because the inputs are inherently noisy, incomplete, or ambiguous.

    • Medical Diagnosis: A collection of patient symptoms (fever, fatigue, cough) can map to dozens of different pathologies. Physicians use clinical heuristics to identify the most probable disease and formulate treatment plans.
    • Computer Vision & Scene Understanding: A 2D photographic projection of a 3D world is mathematically ill-posed and ambiguous. Optical illusions clearly demonstrate that several distinct 3D structures can produce identical 2D images. Computer vision algorithms use geometric and lighting heuristics to pick the most plausible physical interpretation.
  2. Computational Cost & Combinatorial Explosion:

    The problem is well-defined and has an exact solution, but the state space is so gargantuan that exhaustive brute-force search would require centuries and petabytes of memory.

    • Chess: Has an estimated state space of 10120 possible games (Shannon number) with an average branching factor of b ≈ 35. No supercomputer can search to the terminal states.
    • 8-Puzzle & 15-Puzzle: The 15-puzzle has over 1013 reachable states. Uninformed search exhausts memory within seconds.
    • Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP): Visiting n cities involves (n-1)! possible tours, rendering brute-force enumeration impossible for n > 20.
Crucial Concept: Heuristics are Fallible
Because a heuristic relies on incomplete information, intuition, or approximations, heuristics are inherently fallible. They do not guarantee the optimal solution in every single instance unless mathematically proved to possess special properties (such as admissibility in A*). A heuristic is an informed guide, not an infallible oracle.
Real-World Analogy • The Supermarket Checkout Line

Suppose you are at a supermarket with several checkout counters. A simple heuristic is: "Join the lane with the fewest people waiting."

In many situations, this heuristic works well. However, it can fail:

  • The single customer in front of you might have three overflowing carts and price disputes.
  • A lane with four customers might be the "10 items or fewer" express checkout where people clear out in seconds.
  • A lane might appear completely empty simply because the cashier just closed the counter!

This demonstrates that heuristics provide smart guidance, but additional contextual knowledge can drastically refine or override them.

Key Takeaways

  • Blind search explores blindly; heuristic search uses domain knowledge to direct the search toward the goal.
  • Heuristics are required when problems are inherently ambiguous or computationally intractable.
  • Heuristics are fallible rules-of-thumb that trade perfection for computational feasibility.

2Hill Climbing Search (Steepest Ascent)

Greedy local search guided by immediate improvement

Hill Climbing is one of the simplest heuristic search techniques. It is an informed, greedy local search algorithm that operates entirely on local knowledge. At each step, it inspects only the immediate neighboring states and moves to the neighbor that provides the greatest immediate improvement according to an evaluation function.

The Foggy Mountain Analogy (Lecture 7 Slide 13)

Imagine you are hiking up a mountain in dense fog. Visibility is zero; you cannot see the summit or how far away you are. In your pocket, you carry an altimeter that reports your exact elevation.

To reach the summit, you take a step in several directions (North, South, East, West), check the altimeter at each point, and then commit to walking in the direction of steepest upward slope. You repeat this until every direction leads downward, concluding you have reached the top.

Formal Hill Climbing Algorithm (Steepest Ascent)

Algorithm:
  1. Evaluate the initial state s0. If it is a goal, return success. Otherwise, set Current = s0.
  2. Generate all legal successors of Current.
  3. Evaluate each successor state using evaluation function E(s).
  4. Let BestSuccessor be the successor with the highest evaluation score (for maximization problems).
  5. If E(BestSuccessor) ≤ E(Current), terminate and return Current as the solution (or failure).
  6. Otherwise, set Current = BestSuccessor and loop back to Step 2.
Irrevocable Control Strategy
Notice a critical feature of Hill Climbing: Hill Climbing does not maintain an OPEN list or a history of alternate unexpanded branches! Once a move is selected, all alternate paths are discarded forever. In AI literature (Luger Chapter 4.1), this is called an irrevocable control strategy. It uses O(1) memory because it stores only the current state.

3Problems & Pathologies of Hill Climbing

When greedy local search gets trapped

Because hill climbing relies exclusively on myopic local knowledge without looking ahead or maintaining a backtracking memory, it readily fails in complex state spaces. Lecture 7 and Luger Chapter 4.1 categorize the three classical failure modes:

1. The Local Maximum (Foothills)

A local maximum is a peak that is higher than all its neighboring states, but lower than the true global maximum (the optimal goal).

The Trap: Once the algorithm climbs to the summit of a foothill, every step in every direction leads downward (E(neighbor) < E(current)). The algorithm assumes it has reached the top and halts prematurely, trapped far from the actual goal!

Remedy: Random Restart Hill Climbing. Run multiple searches starting from different, randomly generated initial states. If they converge to the same peak, confidence grows that the peak is indeed the global maximum.

2. The Ridge

A ridge is a narrow elevated terrain feature where the spine slopes upward gently toward the summit, but the sides drop off precipitously.

The Trap: If the algorithm is constrained to take orthogonal steps (e.g., North, South, East, West), while the ridge runs diagonally (e.g., North-East), every single orthogonal step off the ridge leads down the steep sides. Consequently, the algorithm halts along the ridge, unable to navigate diagonally!

Remedy: Allow diagonal steps or composite macro-operators that combine multiple atomic moves to traverse along the diagonal spine.

3. The Plateau (Shoulders & Plains)

A plateau is a flat region of the search space where all neighboring states evaluate to the exact same heuristic score.

The Trap: Because all gradients are zero, the evaluation function provides zero direction or guidance. The algorithm wander aimlessly in a random walk or terminates immediately because no successor is strictly superior.

Remedy: Allow sideways moves (stepping to neighbors with equal heuristic value, with a loop limit), or take larger variable step sizes hoping to cross the plain to an upward incline.

Heuristic Pruning in Tic-Tac-Toe (Lecture 7 Slide 11-12)

A simple heuristic in Tic-Tac-Toe is: Choose the move that maximizes the number of remaining open winning lines for player 'X'.

  • Playing a corner square participates in 3 possible winning lines (1 row, 1 col, 1 diagonal).
  • Playing the center square participates in 4 possible winning lines (1 row, 1 col, 2 diagonals).
  • Playing an edge (side) square participates in only 2 possible winning lines (1 row, 1 col).

This heuristic assigns the center square a score of 4, corners a score of 3, and edges a score of 2. Hill climbing immediately selects the center. While effective here, against an active opponent who creates immediate double threats, simple local count heuristics can easily be blinded unless combined with minimax lookahead!

4The Best-First Search Algorithm

Combining heuristic guidance with systematic graph exploration

To overcome the fatal flaw of hill climbing (getting permanently stuck at local maxima because alternatives are discarded), we need an algorithm that:

  1. Uses an evaluation function f(n) to greedily pursue the most promising node.
  2. Preserves all unselected alternative paths in memory so that if the currently explored path deteriorates, the search can seamlessly recover and switch to an alternative branch.

This algorithm is Best-First Search (Luger Chapter 4.2).

The Core Data Structures: OPEN and CLOSED

  • OPEN: A priority queue containing states that have been generated but not yet expanded. States on OPEN are sorted in order of their heuristic evaluation values f(n) (most promising node at the front).
  • CLOSED: A list/set containing states that have already been expanded. CLOSED prevents infinite loops in cyclic graphs by ensuring states are not repeatedly expanded.

Luger's Best-First Search Algorithm (Formal Pseudocode)

function BEST_FIRST_SEARCH(Start_State, Goal_Test)
    1. OPEN := [Start_State]           // Priority queue ordered by heuristic f
       CLOSED := []                    // Visited set initially empty
    
    2. while OPEN is not empty do:
          a. Remove the leftmost (best) state from OPEN; call it n.
          
          b. if Goal_Test(n) = true then:
                return SUCCESS (reconstruct path from Start_State to n)
          
          c. Generate all legal children/successors of n.
          
          d. For each child state of n:
                i. Case 1: child is not on OPEN and not on CLOSED:
                      Assign heuristic evaluation f(child)
                      Add child to OPEN
                
                ii. Case 2: child is already on OPEN:
                      If the new path to child is shorter/better:
                         Update child's parent pointer and f-value on OPEN
                
                iii. Case 3: child is already on CLOSED:
                      If the new path to child is shorter/better:
                         Remove child from CLOSED, update value, re-insert onto OPEN
          
          e. Add n to CLOSED.
          
          f. Re-sort OPEN according to heuristic evaluation f(n).
    
    3. return FAILURE (OPEN is empty and no goal was found)

Step-by-Step Worked Trace from Lecture 7 (Slides 16–17)

Consider a graph starting at node A. We wish to reach a goal. The heuristic function h(n) estimates the remaining distance to the goal:

Iteration Current Node (n) Heuristic h(n) Successors Generated OPEN List (sorted by best h) CLOSED List
Start - - - [A(h=4)] []
Step 1 A 4 B(h=5), C(h=4), D(h=2) [D(2), C(4), B(5)] [A]
Step 2 D (best on OPEN) 2 E(h=3), F(h=4) [E(3), C(4), F(4), B(5)] [D, A]
Step 3 E 3 H(h=6) [C(4), F(4), B(5), H(6)] [E, D, A]
Step 4 C (switches path!) 4 G(h=1) [G(1), F(4), B(5), H(6)] [C, E, D, A]
Step 5 G 1 Goal(h=0) [Goal(0), F(4), B(5), H(6)] [G, C, E, D, A]
Step 6 Goal 0 - Terminates with SUCCESS! [Goal, G, C, E, D, A]
Deep Observation: Dynamic Recovery
Notice what happened at Step 3 and 4! The search pursued branch D → E because D looked very promising (h=2). However, E's child H had a terrible score (h=6). Instead of dying or charging down an awful path like hill climbing would, Best-First Search dynamically shifted its attention back to node C (h=4), which had been patiently waiting on the OPEN list. Node C subsequently led directly to G and the Goal!

5Best-First Search vs. Hill Climbing

Irrevocable local search vs tentative global heuristic search

Students frequently confuse Hill Climbing and Best-First Search because both algorithms greedily prefer nodes with superior evaluation scores. However, their structural differences are profound:

Dimension Hill Climbing (Steepest Ascent) Best-First Search
Control Strategy Irrevocable: Commits to a single successor; completely discards all other generated branches. Tentative: Selects the best state, but saves all alternative states on OPEN for potential future recovery.
Backtracking Capability None. Cannot backtrack or reconsider past decisions once taken. Full. If the current branch deteriorates, the algorithm automatically resumes exploration from the next best state on OPEN.
Next State Selection Only selects a successor if its value is strictly better than the current state (E(next) > E(curr)). Always selects the globally best available state from OPEN, even if that state has a worse value than the state just explored!
Susceptibility to Local Maxima Extremely high. Readily gets trapped on foothills, ridges, and plateaus. Immune to local maxima traps. When a local peak is exhausted, the algorithm drops back to unvisited states on OPEN.
Memory Complexity O(1) (constant space): Stores only the current state. O(bd) (exponential space): Retains all unexpanded boundary nodes in OPEN and expanded nodes in CLOSED.
Completeness Incomplete. Can terminate prematurely without finding a solution even if one exists. Complete on finite graphs. Because it never gives up on a path permanently, it will eventually find a goal if a path exists.

6Summary & Tricky Practice Questions

Mastery review and exam-level critical thinking

Lecture 7 Key Takeaways

  • Heuristics: Informed rules of thumb used to combat inherent ambiguity or combinatorial explosion. They are fallible and do not guarantee optimality by default.
  • Hill Climbing: Greedy, memory-free (O(1)) local search. Prone to 3 pathologies: Local Maxima, Ridges, and Plateaus.
  • Best-First Search: Maintains OPEN (priority queue) and CLOSED (visited set). It combines greedy heuristic evaluation with systematic graph search, ensuring complete exploration on finite graphs.
  • Dynamic Recovery: When a path turns sour, Best-First search automatically switches back to the most promising node remaining on the OPEN list.
High-Yield Exam Practice Questions
Question 1 (Tricky): Can Hill Climbing ever escape a local maximum if allowed infinite iterations?

Answer: Standard deterministic Hill Climbing will never escape a local maximum, even with infinite time, because at a local maximum all neighbors have worse evaluation values. The algorithm terminates immediately at step 5. Only variants with stochastic modifications—such as Random Restart Hill Climbing or Simulated Annealing (which probabilistically accepts downhill moves)—can escape local maxima.

Question 2 (Conceptual): In Best-First Search, why is a CLOSED list strictly necessary? What happens if it is omitted?

Answer: In graphs containing cycles (or reversible operators, e.g., moving an 8-puzzle tile back and forth), omitting CLOSED causes the search to cycle infinitely between mutual parent-child states. Furthermore, multiple paths can reach the same state; CLOSED prevents duplicate exponential re-expansion of previously explored subgraphs.

Question 3 (Critical Thinking): Why does Best-First Search guarantee completeness on finite graphs while Hill Climbing does not?

Answer: Best-First Search never permanently discards any generated state; every unexpanded successor remains stored on OPEN. If a selected path hits a dead end or bad evaluations, the algorithm naturally falls back to the remaining nodes on OPEN. On a finite graph, because duplicate states are placed on CLOSED, the search space is bounded, guaranteeing that if a goal exists, it will eventually be extracted from OPEN.

Question 4 (Analytical): You are on a ridge during Hill Climbing. Explain why testing only North, South, East, and West leads to failure even though an upward path exists.

Answer: If the ridge runs diagonally (e.g., North-East), the upward gradient exists strictly along the diagonal line ec{v} = (1, 1). Taking an orthogonal step (e.g., pure North (0, 1) or pure East (1, 0)) steps off the crest of the ridge into the steep downward drop on either side. Because each individual orthogonal neighbor has a lower elevation than the current crest, Hill Climbing concludes it is at a local peak and halts.

FAST Sessional-I • Feb 2026 Question 1d, 1e, 1f (7 Marks)

Question 1: Manhattan vs Diagonal Heuristic Admissibility & Dominance Proof

SP-2026 Exam Fresh 2026 Paper

Problem Statement: An autonomous delivery robot moves in a 2D warehouse grid with obstacles. Movement rules and costs: Horizontal or Vertical move → cost = 3; Diagonal move → cost = 4. Goal is at (xg, yg).

  1. Is the Manhattan distance heuristic h1(n) = |xg - x| + |yg - y| admissible for this problem? Prove your answer.
  2. Consider another heuristic: h2(n) = 4 × min(dx, dy) + 3 × |dx - dy|, where dx = |xg - x| and dy = |yg - y|. Is h2 admissible? How?
  3. Between h1 and h2, which heuristic will make A* search more efficient? Justify.
Step-by-Step Model Solution & Mathematical Proof

Part 1: Is h1(n) Admissible? NO (INADMISSIBLE!)

Proof by Counterexample: A heuristic is admissible if and only if for all states n, h(n) ≤ h*(n) (it never overestimates the true minimal cost to goal). Let the robot be at n = (3, 2) and goal at (4, 3):

  • True Cost h*(n): The robot can make a single diagonal move from (3,2) to (4,3). The true cost of this move is 4.
  • Manhattan Cost Calculation: Manhattan distance evaluates dx = |4-3| = 1, dy = |3-2| = 1. If Manhattan distance is evaluated in grid step costs: 1 × 3 + 1 × 3 = 6.
  • Because 6 > 4 (estimated cost exceeds true cost), the heuristic overestimates the cost of diagonal shortcuts!
  • Even if evaluated as raw coordinate sum: for start (0,0) to goal (4,3), h_1(0,0) = 4 + 3 = 7, but 3 diagonal moves (cost 12) + 1 horizontal move (cost 3) = 15. However, on any local step where a diagonal move is available, Manhattan assumes 2 orthogonal steps (cost 6), overestimating the direct diagonal option (cost 4). Therefore, h1 is inadmissible.

Part 2: Is h2(n) Admissible? YES (ADMISSIBLE!)

Proof via Relaxed Problem:

  • Consider a relaxed version of the warehouse grid where all obstacle shelves are removed.
  • In this unobstructed grid, the absolute fastest way to travel between (x, y) and (xg, yg) is to make min(dx, dy) diagonal steps (each costing 4) to simultaneously reduce both x and y differences, and then make the remaining |dx - dy| straight orthogonal steps (each costing 3).
  • The total cost of this optimal unobstructed path is precisely:
    h2(n) = 4 × min(dx, dy) + 3 × |dx - dy|
  • In the real warehouse, obstacle shelves can only force the robot to take longer detours, meaning h*(n) ≥ h2(n) for all states.
  • Since the optimal cost in a relaxed problem is always a lower bound on the original problem, h2 is mathematically guaranteed to be admissible.

Part 3: Efficiency Comparison (Dominance):

Answer: h2(n) makes A* strictly more efficient and reliable.

  • First, h1 is inadmissible, so A* using h1 does not even guarantee finding the optimal path.
  • Second, h2 correctly models diagonal movement. Because it provides a tighter, more informed lower bound (h2(n) ≥ 0), it dominates less informed heuristics.
  • A* will expand fewer total nodes with h2 because states with high heuristic values are pruned early, keeping the search focused directly along the optimal diagonal trajectory.
FAST Sessional-I • Feb 2024 Question 1d (Heuristic Justification)

Question 2: Inadmissible Heuristic Formulation in Grid Search

SP-2024 Exam Common Trap

Problem Statement: In the "Solve-It Felix" grid game, each move {L, R, U, D} has an actual cost of 1. A student designs the following heuristic to bias Felix toward vertical movement: h(n) = Horizontal Distance + 5 × Vertical Distance. Is this heuristic admissible? Justify your answer.

Model Solution

Answer: NOT ADMISSIBLE.

Justification: By multiplying vertical distance by 5, the heuristic assumes each vertical step costs 5, whereas the actual move cost in the game is only 1. For any node n that requires vertical travel, h(n) vastly overestimates the true cost.

Concrete Counterexample: Consider a cell G that is 1 cell directly above the key cell (Horizontal = 0, Vertical = 1). The actual minimal cost to reach the key is h*(G) = 1 move. However:

h(G) = 0 + 5 × (1) = 5 > h*(G) = 1
Because h(G) > h*(G), the heuristic overestimates the cost to goal, violating admissibility.

FAST Mid-I • Fall 2017 Problem 2c & 2d (Short Questions)

Question 3: Hill-Climbing vs Best-First Search & Extreme Parameter Cases

CS401 NUCES Conceptual 2-Markers

Problem Statement:

  1. State two major differences between Hill-Climbing Search and Best-First Search.
  2. What algorithm results as a special case if:
    • (i) Local Beam Search is applied with k = 1?
    • (ii) Genetic Algorithm is run with population size N = 1 and a mutation rate of 1.0 (always apply mutation)?
Model Solution

1. Two Major Differences:

Feature Hill-Climbing Search Best-First Search
Memory & Frontier Keeps zero memory of previous states (maintains only current state, O(1) space). No agenda or frontier. Maintains a global priority queue (OPEN list) of all unexplored nodes and a CLOSED list of visited states.
Backtracking & Completeness Cannot backtrack. If it hits a local maximum or shoulder, it terminates or gets stuck forever (incomplete). Can backtrack. If the current branch looks unpromising, it automatically reverts to the next best node on OPEN (complete on finite graphs).

2. Extreme Parameter Special Cases:

  • (i) Local Beam Search with k = 1: Becomes standard Greedy Local Hill-Climbing. (With k=1, exactly one state is maintained at each step, choosing the best neighbor from its immediate successors).
  • (ii) Genetic Algorithm with N = 1 and Mutation Rate = 1: Becomes a Random Walk / Stochastic Local Hill Climbing. (With N=1, no crossover/sexual reproduction is possible. Since mutation occurs with probability 1.0 at every step, the single individual undergoes continuous random point mutations, equivalent to random-walk local search).

FAST Mid-1 Exam Pro-Tips for Informed Search

  1. The Counterexample Technique: When asked to prove a heuristic is NOT admissible, never write general paragraphs. Pick ONE single state, compute h(n), compute the actual cost h*(n), and explicitly write: "Since h(n) > h*(n), it overestimates and is therefore inadmissible." FAST professors award 100% marks immediately for this clean structure.
  2. The Relaxed Problem Technique: To prove a heuristic IS admissible, show that it is the exact optimal solution to a relaxed problem where one or more constraints (walls, rules) are removed.
  3. Heuristic Dominance Definition: For two admissible heuristics h1 and h2, h2 dominates h1 if for all states n, h2(n) ≥ h1(n) and strictly greater for at least one state. A dominating heuristic will never expand more nodes than the dominated one.
